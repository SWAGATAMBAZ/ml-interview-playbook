ML forecasting: lag features, LightGBM, global models & Prophet

Turn a time series into a table — lags, rolling statistics, calendar and price features — and any regressor becomes a forecaster. Gradient boosting trained as one global model across thousands of series is what won the M5 retail competition and what most demand-forecasting teams run in production. This page covers the features, the leakage traps, multi-step strategies, regression with Fourier terms and Prophet.

🧒 In plain English

Classical models look at one shop's sales history and extrapolate. An ML forecaster instead builds a spreadsheet: each row is “one shop on one day”, the columns are clues (sales yesterday, sales same day last week, average of the last 4 weeks, is it a holiday, is there a promotion, what's the price), and the target is that day's sales. Then a gradient-boosting model learns the rules. Because you can stack every shop and every product into one spreadsheet, the model learns patterns shared across them — “promotions lift sales by ~30% in week one” — which a model of one shop could never see.

How it works, step by step

  1. Choose the forecast origin and horizon (e.g. at Monday night, forecast the next 28 days).
  2. Build features using only data available at the origin: lags ≥ horizon (or recursive), rolling windows on shifted data, calendar, known-future covariates, static attributes.
  3. Stack all series into one long table with series identifiers as features (global model).
  4. Train a gradient-boosting model (LightGBM/XGBoost/CatBoost) with a suitable loss (L2, Tweedie, Poisson, quantile).
  5. Produce multi-step forecasts — recursive, direct (one model per horizon) or horizon-as-a-feature.
  6. Backtest with rolling origins, compare against seasonal naive and ETS, and reconcile if hierarchical.
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“I'd frame it as supervised learning: features are lags, rolling means and standard deviations, calendar and holiday flags, price and promotion, and static attributes; the target is the value h steps ahead. I'd train one global LightGBM model across all series with a Tweedie or Poisson loss for sparse non-negative demand, scaling targets per series. The crucial part is leakage control — every feature must be computable at the forecast origin, so lags must be at least the horizon or the model is applied recursively. I'd choose recursive, direct or a multi-horizon setup, validate with rolling-origin backtests against seasonal naive, and remember trees can't extrapolate trends, so I'd detrend or model ratios.”

Tabularising a series

Sliding window: series → (features, target) rows
Lookback L becomes the feature columns; the next H values are the targets. Every shift of the window is one training row.

A series of length T with lookback L and horizon H gives T − L − H + 1 rows per series. With 30,000 series × 1,900 days (M5) that's tens of millions of rows — exactly what gradient boosting handles well.

The leakage traps (the #1 reason ML forecasts look great offline)

  • Rolling features not shifted: rolling(7).mean() includes today's target. Always shift(1) (or shift by h) before rolling, and group by series.
  • Lags shorter than the horizon in a direct model: to forecast 14 days ahead you don't know yesterday's value.
  • Random K-fold CV: future rows train the model that predicts the past. Use rolling-origin splits, with a gap if data arrive late.
  • Global statistics (scaling, target encoding, imputation) computed over the whole history including the test period.
  • Covariates with hindsight: actual weather, final revised figures, stock-outs only recorded later, promotions decided after the fact.
  • Survivorship: training only on products that still exist today.

Neither recursive nor direct wins universally; with linear dynamics they're often similar, with nonlinear models and long horizons direct is usually safer. M5 winners used variants of both. Always report error by horizon.

Local vs global models

Global (one model, all series)

  • Learns shared patterns (seasonality shapes, promo lift, holiday effects)
  • Works for short and new series (cold start via static features)
  • One model to train, monitor and deploy
  • Cross-learning reduces variance

Local (one model per series)

  • Fits series-specific dynamics exactly
  • Robust when series are unrelated
  • Easy to parallelise and interpret
  • Classical ETS/ARIMA are local; thousands of fits to maintain

Making global models work: scale each series (divide by its recent mean or use log/ratio targets) so big and small series share patterns; add series IDs and attributes as categoricals; weight series by business value; consider separate models per category if series are very heterogeneous.

Lessons from the M5 competition

In M5, which used Walmart sales of 3,049 products across 10 stores, almost all of the top 50 methods used LightGBM, exploiting cross-series learning on tens of thousands of hierarchical series. Practical takeaways:

  • Global gradient boosting with Tweedie/Poisson loss on raw counts or scaled targets.
  • Features: lags at 28+ days for the direct horizon, rolling means/std, price features, calendar and SNAP/event flags.
  • Ensembles of models trained per store or per category and with different seeds/horizons.
  • Noise grows as you drill down the hierarchy — item-store level is extremely intermittent, so aggregate levels are more predictable.
  • Weighted, scaled metrics (WRMSSE) match business value better than plain RMSE.

Regression with Fourier terms (dynamic harmonic regression)

∑ Smooth seasonality with a few columns
$$y_t=\beta_0+\beta_1t+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big[a_k\sin\frac{2\pi kt}{P}+b_k\cos\frac{2\pi kt}{P}\Big]+\text{holidays}+\eta_t$$

K pairs approximate any smooth periodic shape with period P (even non-integer, e.g. 365.25). Small K = smooth, large K = wiggly; choose K by AICc or CV. Add several periods for multiple seasonality, and ARIMA errors for the remaining autocorrelation.

Prophet

∑ Prophet's additive model
$$y(t)=g(t)+s(t)+h(t)+\varepsilon_t$$

g = piecewise-linear (or logistic, with a capacity) trend with automatically placed changepoints; s = Fourier-series seasonalities (yearly, weekly, daily, custom); h = holiday effects with windows. It's a regression on time, fitted by MAP with Stan.

  • Strengths: easy for analysts, handles missing data and outliers, multiple seasonalities, holidays, interpretable components plots, uncertainty from trend changes.
  • Weaknesses: treats time as a regressor and ignores autocorrelation (no lags), so short-term accuracy is often worse than ETS/ARIMA; trend extrapolation can go wild; benchmark studies often find it beaten by simple baselines.
  • Key knobs: changepoint_prior_scale (trend flexibility — the most important), seasonality_prior_scale, seasonality_mode ('additive'/'multiplicative'), holidays, growth='logistic' with cap.
  • NeuralProphet adds autoregressive lags (AR-Net) and lagged regressors.

Must-know points for interviews

  • Tabularise: lags, rolling stats (shifted!), calendar, events, prices, static attributes.
  • Leakage is the biggest risk: every feature must exist at the forecast origin.
  • Multi-step: recursive vs direct vs MIMO; report error per horizon.
  • Global models share information across series — the M5 lesson; scale targets per series.
  • Loss choice: Tweedie/Poisson for sparse counts, quantile for service levels.
  • Trees can't extrapolate: detrend, difference or predict ratios.
  • Prophet = trend + Fourier seasonality + holidays regression; easy, not autoregressive, often not the most accurate.

Interview questions

How do you use a regression model like XGBoost for forecasting?

Convert the series into a supervised table: for each time t, features from information available at the origin (lags, rolling statistics, calendar, events, known-future covariates, static attributes) and the target y at t + h. Train with time-ordered validation, then produce multi-step forecasts recursively or with direct models per horizon.

Recursive vs direct multi-step forecasting?

Recursive trains one one-step model and feeds predictions back as inputs: simple and uses recent lags, but errors compound and there's a train/serve mismatch. Direct trains a model per horizon on lags ≥ h: no compounding, but more models, less recent information for long horizons and possibly inconsistent paths. Hybrids (DirRec, MIMO, horizon-as-feature) sit in between. Choose by backtest error per horizon.

What is a global forecasting model and why does it often win?

One model trained on many series stacked together, with series identifiers/attributes as features. It learns shared patterns (seasonal shapes, promo and holiday effects), has far more training data per parameter (lower variance), handles short and new series, and is one artefact to maintain. It wins when series are related; per-series scaling is key.

Give examples of leakage in time-series feature engineering.

Rolling means that include the current target (no shift), lags shorter than the horizon in direct models, target encodings or scalers computed on the full history, random K-fold CV, covariates known only in hindsight (actual weather, revised data, recorded stock-outs), and back-filled missing values from the future.

How do tree-based forecasters handle trend?

Poorly — they can't predict beyond the training range. Fixes: difference the target or predict the ratio to a recent level, detrend with a linear model and let trees model residuals, use LightGBM's linear_tree, or normalise each window by its last value/mean (as deep models do with RevIN).

Why use a Tweedie loss for retail demand?

Item-level demand is non-negative, right-skewed and has many zeros. Tweedie with variance power between 1 and 2 is a compound Poisson-Gamma distribution: a point mass at zero plus a continuous positive part. It models that shape directly (with a log link), unlike L2 loss which assumes symmetric errors and can predict negatives.

What did the M5 competition teach practitioners?

Global LightGBM models with rich lag, rolling, price and calendar features dominated the top of the leaderboard; cross-learning across tens of thousands of related series beat per-series statistical models; ensembles and careful validation mattered; lower levels of the hierarchy are much noisier; and the metric (weighted, scaled) shapes the right loss and weighting.

How does Prophet work, and what are its limitations?

It fits y = trend + seasonality + holidays + error: a piecewise-linear or logistic trend with changepoints, Fourier-series seasonalities and holiday windows, by MAP estimation. It's easy, robust to missing data and interpretable. But it has no autoregressive terms, so it ignores short-term dynamics; it can extrapolate trends badly; and benchmarks often show it underperforming ETS/ARIMA or even seasonal naive. Tune changepoint_prior_scale and validate by backtesting.

What are Fourier terms and when would you use them?

Pairs sin(2πkt/P), cos(2πkt/P) for k = 1…K that approximate a smooth periodic pattern of period P. They handle long (365) or non-integer (52.18) periods with few parameters and allow several seasonalities at once — in linear regression, ARIMA errors (dynamic harmonic regression), Prophet, or as features for ML models.

How would you forecast a brand-new product with no history?

A global model with static features (category, price tier, brand, launch season) and age-since-launch as a feature, so it learns typical launch curves from similar past products; analogous-product methods (average the scaled curves of the k most similar launches); Bass diffusion models; or judgemental adjustments. Update quickly as the first weeks of data arrive and use wide intervals.

How do you validate an ML forecaster properly?

Rolling-origin backtesting: several cut-off dates, at each one build features using only data up to the cut-off (including covariates as known then), train or reuse the model, forecast the full horizon, and score per horizon and per segment against seasonal naive/ETS. Add a gap if data arrive with delay. Use the same procedure for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping.

Should you include the series ID as a feature in a global model?

Usually yes, as a categorical (LightGBM/CatBoost handle high cardinality reasonably) so the model can learn series-specific offsets — but rely mainly on scaled targets and descriptive attributes so the model generalises to new series. With very many IDs, regularise (min_data_per_group, cat_smooth) or use target-encoded statistics computed in the past.
