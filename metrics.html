∑ Core formulas
$$\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{N}\quad \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\quad \text{Recall (TPR, sensitivity)}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$ $$\text{Specificity (TNR)}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}\quad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=1-\text{specificity}\quad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}\quad F_\beta=\frac{(1+\beta^2)PR}{\beta^2P+R}$$
🧒 In plain English

Precision: of everything I flagged, how much was really positive? (“Don't cry wolf.”) Recall: of all real positives, how many did I catch? (“Don't miss the wolf.”) Spam filters care about precision (don't lose real mail); cancer screening cares about recall (don't miss a tumour).

ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC

  • ROC curve: TPR vs FPR across all thresholds. AUC = probability that a random positive is scored above a random negative (equivalent to the Mann–Whitney U statistic). 0.5 = random, 1 = perfect. Threshold- and scale-invariant (a ranking metric).
  • PR curve: precision vs recall. Average precision (PR-AUC); baseline = prevalence (e.g. 0.01 for 1% positives).
  • With heavy imbalance, ROC-AUC can look great because FPR stays tiny when TN is huge — e.g. 1,000 false positives among 1,000,000 negatives is FPR 0.001 but may swamp 100 true positives. PR-AUC exposes this. Use PR-AUC when positives are rare and you care about the positive class.
  • ROC-AUC doesn't change if you rescale scores; it says nothing about calibration.

Probabilistic metrics & calibration

  • Log-loss: \(-\frac1n\sum[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)]\) — punishes confident errors; proper scoring rule.
  • Brier score: \(\frac1n\sum(p-y)^2\) — MSE of probabilities; decomposes into calibration + refinement.
  • Calibration: among cases predicted 0.7, ~70% should be positive. Check with a reliability diagram / ECE. Fix with Platt scaling (sigmoid) or isotonic regression (CalibratedClassifierCV) on held-out data.
  • Calibration matters whenever probabilities feed decisions: expected-value pricing, risk scores, thresholds based on costs.

RMSE vs MAE: RMSE ≥ MAE always; a large gap means a few big errors. Choose RMSE when big errors are disproportionately costly, MAE when all errors cost linearly or with outliers.

Ranking & recommendation metrics

  • Precision@k, Recall@k, Hit rate@k
  • MAP (mean average precision): rewards relevant items ranked early.
  • MRR: mean of 1/rank of the first relevant item.
  • NDCG@k: graded relevance with log-discount \(\sum\frac{2^{rel_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)}\), normalised by the ideal ranking.
  • Beyond accuracy: coverage, diversity, novelty, and online metrics (CTR, conversion, dwell time).

Choosing a metric (the interview framework)

  1. What decision does the model drive, and what does each error type cost? Translate to a cost matrix if possible.
  2. Is the positive class rare? → PR-AUC / F1 / recall at fixed precision.
  3. Ranking or thresholded decision? → AUC/NDCG vs precision/recall at an operating point.
  4. Do probabilities matter? → log-loss, Brier, calibration.
  5. Are there capacity constraints? → precision@k.
  6. Pick one primary metric + guardrail metrics (latency, fairness across segments, calibration).

“For fraud with 0.2% positives and an ops team that can review 500 cases a day, I'd optimise PR-AUC during model selection, then choose the threshold that gives the top 500 scores and report precision@500 and the fraud value caught, with false-positive rate as a guardrail for customer friction.”

Interview questions

Explain precision and recall with an example.

Precision = TP/(TP+FP): of the transactions flagged as fraud, the fraction actually fraudulent. Recall = TP/(TP+FN): of all fraudulent transactions, the fraction we flagged. Raising the threshold increases precision and lowers recall; the right balance depends on the costs of false alarms vs misses.

Why is accuracy a bad metric for imbalanced data?

With 1% positives, predicting “negative” for everyone gives 99% accuracy while catching nothing. Accuracy is dominated by the majority class; use precision/recall, F1, PR-AUC, balanced accuracy or MCC, or a cost-based metric.

What does ROC-AUC mean intuitively?

The probability that the model scores a randomly chosen positive higher than a randomly chosen negative. It summarises ranking quality across all thresholds; 0.5 is random, 1.0 perfect. It is insensitive to class balance and to monotonic transformations of scores.

When would you prefer PR-AUC over ROC-AUC?

When positives are rare and you care about performance on them (fraud, disease, defects). ROC-AUC uses FPR, whose denominator (all negatives) is huge, so many false positives barely move it; PR-AUC uses precision, which directly reflects false alarms relative to true detections.

What is the F1 score and when is it not appropriate?

The harmonic mean of precision and recall, high only when both are high. Not appropriate when costs of FP and FN differ a lot (use Fβ or a cost function), when true negatives matter, or when you need threshold-free comparison (use AUCs).

Macro vs micro vs weighted averaging?

Macro: average per-class metrics equally — highlights minority-class performance. Weighted: average weighted by class support. Micro: aggregate TP/FP/FN across classes — dominated by large classes; equals accuracy for single-label multiclass.

What is model calibration and how do you check it?

A model is calibrated if predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (of cases predicted 0.8, ~80% are positive). Check with reliability diagrams, Expected Calibration Error, Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data. SVMs, Naive Bayes, boosted trees and class-weighted models often need it.

RMSE vs MAE — when to use which?

RMSE squares errors, so it penalises large errors more and corresponds to predicting the mean; use it when big misses are especially costly. MAE treats all errors linearly, is robust to outliers and corresponds to the median. Report both: a big RMSE/MAE gap signals a few large errors.

Type I vs Type II error?

Type I = false positive (rejecting a true null; flagging a legit transaction). Type II = false negative (failing to reject a false null; missing fraud). In hypothesis testing α controls Type I; power = 1 − β where β is the Type II rate.

How do you choose a classification threshold?

Not 0.5 by default. Options: maximise F1/Fβ on validation; minimise expected cost using a cost matrix (threshold = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) for calibrated probabilities); meet a constraint (precision ≥ 90%, or top-k capacity); Youden's J on ROC. Choose on validation data and confirm on test.

What is log-loss and why might it be preferred to accuracy?

The negative mean log-likelihood of true labels under predicted probabilities. It evaluates probability quality, heavily penalises confident wrong predictions, is a proper scoring rule (optimised by true probabilities), and is differentiable — accuracy ignores confidence entirely.

Your model has 0.95 ROC-AUC but the business says it's useless. Why might that be?

Extreme imbalance (precision at usable thresholds is tiny), evaluation on the wrong population/time period (leakage or drift), metric misaligned with the decision (they need top-k precision or calibrated risk), poor performance on the segment that matters, or predictions that aren't actionable/come too late.

What is NDCG?

Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain: sums graded relevance of ranked items discounted by log₂(position + 1), divided by the ideal DCG so it lies in [0, 1]. It rewards putting highly relevant items at the top; used for search and recommendation ranking evaluation (NDCG@k).

What is MCC and why is it useful?

Matthews Correlation Coefficient = (TP·TN − FP·FN)/√((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)), ranging −1 to 1. It uses all four confusion-matrix cells and is high only if the classifier does well on both classes — a reliable single metric under imbalance.
