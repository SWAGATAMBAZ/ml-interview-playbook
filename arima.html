ARIMA, SARIMA & SARIMAX

The most-asked classical forecasting model. ARIMA models a series by its own past values (AR), its past forecast errors (MA), after differencing (I) to make it stationary. SARIMA adds seasonal terms; SARIMAX adds external regressors such as price, promotions or weather. This page also covers order selection, diagnostics, VAR, GARCH and the state-space/Kalman view.

🧒 In plain English

Tomorrow's temperature is a lot like today's, a bit like yesterday's (AR: autoregressive — the series remembers its own past). If the weather forecaster under-predicted today, they'll partly correct tomorrow (MA: moving average of past surprises). And if the series keeps climbing (like a city's population), ARIMA first looks at the changes instead of the levels (I: integrated, i.e. differencing), because changes are steadier and easier to model.

SARIMA does the same for “this time last year” (seasonal lags). SARIMAX also lets you say “and a promotion adds 12 units” — external drivers (X).

The Box–Jenkins workflow

  1. Plot & transform: look for trend, seasonality, changing variance; log/Box-Cox if variance grows with level.
  2. Make stationary: difference (d, and seasonal D) until the ADF test rejects a unit root / KPSS doesn't reject stationarity. Don't over-difference.
  3. Identify: read the ACF and PACF of the differenced series for candidate p, q (and P, Q).
  4. Estimate: fit candidates by maximum likelihood; compare AICc/BIC (or let auto-ARIMA search).
  5. Diagnose: residuals should be white noise — Ljung–Box test, residual ACF, normality, no pattern.
  6. Forecast & validate: rolling-origin backtest against seasonal naive and ETS; check interval coverage.
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“ARIMA(p, d, q) differences the series d times to make it stationary, then models it as a linear combination of p past values and q past forecast errors. I pick d with ADF/KPSS tests, p from where the PACF cuts off and q from where the ACF cuts off, then compare candidates by AICc and check that residuals are white noise with Ljung–Box. SARIMA adds seasonal terms (P, D, Q)ₘ; SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors — effectively a regression with ARIMA errors, which needs future values of the regressors at forecast time. It's a strong choice for single series with stable linear autocorrelation, but it's univariate, linear, and slow to scale to thousands of series.”

AR(p): the series remembers its past

∑ Autoregression
$$y_t=c+\phi_1y_{t-1}+\dots+\phi_py_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim\text{white noise}$$

AR(1) with |φ| < 1 is stationary and mean-reverting towards \(\mu=c/(1-\phi)\). φ = 1 is a random walk (unit root, non-stationary). φ close to 1 means slow decay of shocks; negative φ makes the series zig-zag.

Worked example. AR(1): \(y_t=10+0.6\,y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\); mean μ = 10/(1 − 0.6) = 25. Last value y = 30. Forecasts: ŷ₁ = 10 + 0.6·30 = 28, ŷ₂ = 10 + 0.6·28 = 26.8, ŷ₃ = 26.08… converging geometrically to 25 — the classic “ARIMA forecasts flatten out” behaviour.

MA(q): the series remembers past surprises

∑ Moving-average model (not a moving average smoother!)
$$y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\dots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}$$

A shock affects exactly q future periods, then is forgotten: the ACF is zero after lag q. MA models are always stationary; invertibility (|θ| < 1 for MA(1)) ensures they can be rewritten as an AR(∞) and the parameters are unique.

Worked example. MA(1): \(y_t=50+\varepsilon_t+0.4\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Today's surprise was ε = +5. Tomorrow's forecast = 50 + 0.4·5 = 52. Two days ahead = 50 (the shock is forgotten after one step).

ARMA, backshift notation, stationarity

Using the backshift operator \(By_t=y_{t-1}\): \(\phi(B)(1-B)^d y_t=c+\theta(B)\varepsilon_t\) with \(\phi(B)=1-\phi_1B-\dots-\phi_pB^p\) and \(\theta(B)=1+\theta_1B+\dots\). Stationarity requires the roots of φ(B) to lie outside the unit circle; invertibility requires the same for θ(B). Differencing is \((1-B)\); seasonal differencing is \((1-B^m)\).

I: differencing and unit roots

  • First difference \(y_t-y_{t-1}\) removes a stochastic or linear trend. Second difference removes a quadratic trend (rarely needed; d > 2 almost never).
  • Seasonal difference \(y_t-y_{t-m}\) removes a stable seasonal pattern.
  • ADF test: H₀ = unit root (non-stationary). Small p → stationary. KPSS: H₀ = stationary. Use both: they can disagree on borderline series.
  • Over-differencing creates artificial negative autocorrelation (lag-1 ACF near −0.5) and inflates variance. Warning sign: an MA coefficient near −1 after differencing.
  • Trend-stationary vs difference-stationary: a deterministic trend + stationary noise should be detrended (regression on t), not differenced; a random walk with drift should be differenced.

In practice, ACF/PACF reading gives candidates; information criteria decide: AIC = −2 log L + 2k, AICc corrects for small samples, BIC = −2 log L + k log n penalises complexity more and tends to choose smaller, often truer models. Auto-ARIMA (pmdarima, statsforecast's AutoARIMA, R's auto.arima) uses unit-root tests for d and D and a stepwise AICc search over p, q, P, Q. Never compare AIC across different d — the likelihoods are on different data.

SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)ₘ

∑ Multiplicative seasonal ARIMA
$$\Phi(B^m)\,\phi(B)\,(1-B)^d(1-B^m)^D\,y_t=\Theta(B^m)\,\theta(B)\,\varepsilon_t$$

The classic “airline model” is ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)₁₂ on log passengers: one regular and one seasonal difference, one regular and one seasonal MA term. Its forecasts behave much like Holt-Winters.

  • D is rarely more than 1; d + D ≤ 2 in practice.
  • Long seasons (m = 365 daily, m = 168 hourly) make SARIMA slow and unstable — use Fourier terms as exogenous regressors (dynamic harmonic regression) instead.
  • Only one seasonal period is supported.

SARIMAX and regression with ARIMA errors

∑ Regression with ARIMA errors (what statsmodels' SARIMAX fits)
$$y_t=\beta_0+\beta^\top x_t+\eta_t,\qquad \eta_t\sim\text{ARIMA}(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m$$

The β's keep their regression meaning (“a promotion adds 12 units”), while the ARIMA error soaks up the autocorrelation that ordinary regression would wrongly ignore (which makes OLS standard errors too small).

  • The catch: to forecast you need the future values of x. Known-future covariates (calendar, planned prices, promotions, holidays) are fine. Unknown ones (weather, competitor price) must themselves be forecast, which adds error — or be used only lagged.
  • Lagged effects: include x_{t−1}, x_{t−2} or distributed lags when the effect is delayed (advertising carry-over).
  • Fourier terms as X: \(\sin(2\pi kt/m),\cos(2\pi kt/m)\) for k = 1…K handle long or multiple seasonalities with ARIMA errors.
  • Intervention analysis: step or pulse dummies for known events (policy change, outage, COVID lockdown).
  • Strictly, an ARIMAX model puts x inside the ARMA equation, which makes β hard to interpret; regression with ARIMA errors is usually what people mean and want.

Diagnostics: is the model done?

  • Residual ACF: no significant spikes.
  • Ljung–Box test: \(Q=n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{h}\frac{\hat\rho_k^2}{n-k}\sim\chi^2_{h-p-q}\). H₀ = residuals are independent. Small p-value → structure left, revise the model.
  • Residual mean ≈ 0 (bias) and roughly constant variance (else transform or GARCH).
  • Normality only matters for analytic intervals; use bootstrapped intervals otherwise.
  • Out-of-sample: rolling-origin backtest vs baselines. Good residuals don't guarantee good forecasts.

Forecast behaviour and prediction intervals

How uncertainty grows with the horizon
Compare a random walk (variance grows ∝ h), a stationary AR(1) (intervals level off), drift and Holt.
  • Stationary ARMA forecasts revert to the mean and intervals converge to the unconditional variance.
  • With d = 1, intervals grow without bound (∝ √h for a random walk).
  • Analytic intervals assume normal errors and known parameters, so they're usually too narrow; check coverage in backtests and consider bootstrap or conformal intervals (see advanced topics).

When NOT to use ARIMA

  • Strong nonlinear patterns or regime switches (thresholds, saturation) — ARIMA is linear.
  • Multiple seasonalities (hourly data with daily and weekly cycles) — SARIMA supports only one.
  • Thousands of related series — fitting and maintaining one model per series is slow, and short series can't borrow strength; a global gradient-boosting or deep model shares information.
  • Intermittent demand (lots of zeros) — use Croston/TSB or Tweedie models.
  • Very short history — too few points to estimate orders reliably; use simple ETS, a global model or judgement.
  • Rich covariates with complex interactions — tree models handle them more naturally.

Must-know points for interviews

  • p AR lags (PACF cuts off), d differences (ADF/KPSS), q MA lags (ACF cuts off).
  • Stationarity is needed for ARMA; difference trends, seasonally difference seasons; don't over-difference.
  • AICc/BIC to choose orders; never compare across different d.
  • Ljung–Box on residuals: white noise means the model has captured the autocorrelation.
  • SARIMA adds (P, D, Q)ₘ for one seasonal period.
  • SARIMAX = regression with ARIMA errors; needs future covariate values.
  • Forecasts revert to the mean (stationary) or follow a drift; intervals widen with the horizon.
  • Limits: linear, univariate, one seasonality, per-series fitting.

Interview questions

What do p, d and q mean in ARIMA?

p = number of autoregressive lags (past values), d = number of differences applied to make the series stationary, q = number of moving-average lags (past forecast errors). ARIMA(1,1,1) models the first difference as one AR and one MA term.

Why does ARIMA need stationarity?

ARMA estimation assumes the mean, variance and autocovariances are constant over time, so the relationships learned from the past apply to the future and parameters are identifiable. Non-stationary series (trends, unit roots) give spurious relationships and unstable estimates; differencing removes stochastic trends.

How do you choose p and q?

From the stationary series: PACF cutting off after lag p suggests AR(p); ACF cutting off after lag q suggests MA(q); both tailing off suggests ARMA. Then fit several candidates and choose by AICc/BIC, confirm residuals are white noise (Ljung–Box), and compare out-of-sample with a rolling backtest. Auto-ARIMA automates the search.

ADF vs KPSS?

ADF's null is a unit root (non-stationary); rejecting it suggests stationarity. KPSS's null is stationarity; rejecting it suggests a unit root. Using both helps: ADF rejects + KPSS doesn't → stationary; ADF doesn't reject + KPSS rejects → difference; conflicting → possibly trend-stationary or borderline, consider detrending or fractional behaviour.

What happens if you over-difference?

You introduce a non-invertible MA component: lag-1 autocorrelation of about −0.5, an MA coefficient near −1, and larger forecast variance. Check the ACF after differencing and prefer the smallest d that achieves stationarity.

What is the difference between SARIMA and SARIMAX?

SARIMA models a single series using its own past and seasonal lags. SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors — in statsmodels, as a regression whose errors follow a SARIMA process. It captures known drivers (price, promotions, holidays, weather), but you must supply their future values when forecasting.

Your SARIMAX uses temperature as a regressor. What's the problem at forecast time?

Future temperature is unknown: you'd have to plug in a weather forecast, whose errors propagate (and backtests that used actual temperatures are optimistic). Options: use the weather forecast available at each origin in the backtest too, use lagged temperature only, use climatological averages, or produce scenario forecasts. Always backtest with the covariate information that would really be available.

What does the Ljung–Box test check?

Whether a set of residual autocorrelations up to lag h are jointly zero. H₀: residuals are independently distributed (white noise). A small p-value means the model has left autocorrelation unexplained — revise the orders or add terms. Degrees of freedom should be reduced by the number of fitted ARMA parameters.

Why do ARIMA forecasts become flat?

For a stationary ARMA, the effect of the last observed values and errors decays geometrically, so forecasts converge to the unconditional mean. With d = 1 and no drift they converge to a constant level; with drift, to a straight line. MA terms affect only the first q steps. Long-horizon ARIMA forecasts carry little information beyond the mean/trend.

AIC vs BIC for order selection?

AIC = −2 log L + 2k targets predictive accuracy and tends to choose slightly larger models; AICc corrects AIC for small samples; BIC = −2 log L + k ln n penalises parameters more and is consistent (selects the true model as n grows, if it's in the set). Compare only models with the same d and the same data.

How do you model multiple seasonalities with ARIMA?

SARIMA handles one seasonal period. Use dynamic harmonic regression: add Fourier terms for each period (e.g. 24 and 168 for hourly data) as exogenous regressors and let ARIMA errors handle short-term dynamics; choose the number of Fourier pairs by AICc. Alternatives: MSTL + ARIMA on the remainder, TBATS.

What is a Kalman filter and how does it relate to ARIMA?

A recursive algorithm for linear Gaussian state-space models: it predicts the hidden state and its uncertainty, then updates them when a new observation arrives, weighting by the relative uncertainties (the Kalman gain). ARIMA and ETS can be written in state-space form; statsmodels fits SARIMAX by computing the likelihood with the Kalman filter, which also handles missing observations naturally.

When would you use VAR?

When several stationary series influence each other (e.g. interest rates, inflation, unemployment) and you want joint forecasts, Granger-causality tests or impulse-response analysis. Each variable is regressed on lags of all variables; parameters grow as k²p, so keep k small or regularise. For cointegrated non-stationary series use VECM.

What is GARCH used for?

Modelling time-varying volatility: the conditional variance depends on past squared shocks and past variance (σ²ₜ = ω + αε²ₜ₋₁ + βσ²ₜ₋₁). It captures volatility clustering in financial returns; the mean may be modelled separately (often near zero for returns) and GARCH used for risk measures and interval widths.

When would you not use ARIMA?

Strongly nonlinear or regime-switching dynamics, multiple seasonalities, many related series that benefit from a shared model, intermittent demand, very short series, or complex covariate interactions. Prefer ETS/Theta for simple robust baselines, global LightGBM for many series with covariates, Croston/TSB for intermittent demand, and deep models for large multivariate datasets.
