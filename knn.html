k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN)

The “lazy learner”: no training, just memorise the data and, at prediction time, let the k closest points vote. Interviewers use kNN to test your grasp of distance metrics, scaling, the curse of dimensionality and the bias–variance effect of k.

🎯 Say this in the interview

“kNN is a non-parametric, instance-based method: to predict a point, find the k nearest training points under a distance metric (usually Euclidean on standardised features) and take a majority vote (classification) or average (regression), optionally weighted by 1/distance. Training is O(1), prediction is O(n·d) per query unless we use KD-trees, ball trees or approximate nearest-neighbour indexes. Small k → low bias/high variance; large k → smoother, higher bias. It needs feature scaling, suffers from the curse of dimensionality and irrelevant features, and k is chosen by cross-validation.”

How it works

  1. Store the training set (that's “training”).
  2. For a query x, compute distances to all stored points.
  3. Take the k smallest.
  4. Classification: majority (or distance-weighted) vote; class probabilities = vote shares. Regression: (weighted) mean of their targets.
🧒 In plain English

To guess the price of a flat, look at the 5 most similar flats that sold recently (same area, size, floor) and average their prices. “Similar” is the distance metric — and it only works if the features are on comparable scales.

⚠️ Scaling is mandatory

If income is in rupees (0–10,00,000) and age in years (18–80), Euclidean distance is almost entirely income. Standardise or min–max scale — inside a Pipeline.

kNN decision regions
k = 1 memorises every point (training accuracy 100%, wiggly islands). Increase k and the boundary smooths out.

Choosing k (bias–variance)

  • k = 1: zero training error, very high variance, sensitive to noise/outliers. Asymptotic error ≤ 2× the Bayes error (Cover & Hart).
  • k = n: always predicts the majority class / global mean → maximum bias.
  • Use odd k for binary classification to avoid ties; a common starting point is \(k\approx\sqrt n\); tune by CV (plot validation error vs k).
  • Distance weighting (weights="distance") lets you use a larger k while keeping close points more influential.

The curse of dimensionality

As dimensions grow, data become sparse and distances concentrate: the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbour distances approaches 1, so “nearest” stops meaning “similar”. To capture a fixed fraction r of uniformly distributed data in a d-dimensional unit cube you need a neighbourhood with edge length \(r^{1/d}\) — for 10% of the data in 10-D that is 0.79 of each axis. Fixes: feature selection, PCA/embeddings before kNN, metric learning, or use a different model.

Making it fast & robust

  • Prediction cost: brute force O(n·d) per query. KD-trees/ball trees give ~O(log n) in low dimensions. At scale use approximate nearest neighbours: FAISS, HNSW (hnswlib), Annoy, ScaNN — the backbone of vector search and RAG retrieval.
  • Reduce n: condensed / edited nearest neighbours, prototypes. Reduce d: PCA, feature selection.
  • Imbalanced data: majority classes dominate votes — use distance weighting, resampling or class-aware thresholds.
  • Missing values: KNNImputer uses the same idea to fill gaps.

Pros, cons & when to use

✅ Strengths

  • Simple, no training, naturally multiclass
  • Non-linear, flexible boundaries
  • Easy to update — just add data
  • Good for recommendation/similarity search, small low-dimensional data, imputation

❌ Weaknesses

  • Slow, memory-heavy prediction
  • Needs scaling; sensitive to irrelevant features and noise
  • Curse of dimensionality
  • No model/coefficients to interpret globally

Interview questions

Why is kNN called a lazy learner?

It does no generalisation during training — it just stores the data. All computation (distance calculations, voting) happens at prediction time. Contrast with eager learners (e.g., logistic regression) that build a model up front and discard the training data.

How does the choice of k affect the model?

Small k → flexible, low bias, high variance (overfits noise; k = 1 has zero training error). Large k → smoother, higher bias, lower variance; k = n predicts the majority class. Choose by cross-validation; prefer odd k for binary problems.

Why do you need feature scaling for kNN?

Distances are dominated by features with the largest ranges, so unscaled kNN effectively ignores small-range features. Standardisation or min–max scaling gives each feature comparable influence. Fit the scaler on training data only.

What is the curse of dimensionality and how does it affect kNN?

In high dimensions data become sparse and pairwise distances concentrate (nearest ≈ farthest), so neighbourhoods are no longer local and the vote is close to random; you need exponentially more data to keep the same density. Mitigate with feature selection, dimensionality reduction (PCA, embeddings) or metric learning.

What is the time and space complexity of kNN?

Training O(1) time (O(n·d) memory to store data). Brute-force prediction O(n·d) per query plus O(n log k) to select neighbours. KD-trees/ball trees cut queries to ~O(log n) in low dimensions but degrade toward brute force as d grows; approximate methods (HNSW, FAISS) trade exactness for speed.

How does kNN handle categorical features and missing values?

Categoricals need encoding (one-hot) or a suitable metric (Hamming, Gower for mixed types). Missing values must be imputed first (or use a distance that ignores missing coordinates, like sklearn's nan_euclidean used by KNNImputer).

kNN for regression — how?

Predict the (optionally distance-weighted) mean of the k neighbours' targets. Predictions are piecewise constant and can't extrapolate beyond the range of training targets.

How do you choose the distance metric?

By data type and CV: Euclidean for scaled continuous data, Manhattan for high-dimensional or outlier-prone data, cosine for text/embeddings, Hamming/Jaccard for binary/sets, Mahalanobis for correlated features, Gower for mixed data.

What is the asymptotic error of 1-NN?

Cover & Hart (1967): as n → ∞, the 1-NN error rate is at most twice the Bayes (optimal) error. With k → ∞ and k/n → 0, kNN is consistent (approaches Bayes error).

kNN vs K-Means?

Different problems: kNN is supervised (classification/regression) using k labelled neighbours; K-Means is unsupervised clustering into K groups by iteratively updating centroids. Both rely on distance and need scaling.

Where is kNN used in practice today?

Similarity search and recommendations (“customers like you”), vector databases / RAG retrieval with approximate NN, imputation (KNNImputer), anomaly detection (distance to k-th neighbour, LOF), and as a quick baseline on small datasets.

How does kNN deal with imbalanced classes?

Poorly by default — the majority class tends to win the vote in overlap regions. Use distance weighting, resampling, class-weighted votes, or tune a threshold on the vote fraction (predict_proba).
