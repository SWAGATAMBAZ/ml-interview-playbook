AdaBoost (adaptive boosting)

AdaBoost builds a strong classifier from very weak ones — usually one-split “stumps” — by re-weighting the training rows each round so the next stump focuses on the rows the team still gets wrong. It's the cleanest way to understand boosting, and interviewers love it because every number can be computed by hand.

🧒 In plain English

A teacher has a class of not-very-clever students, each of whom only knows one simple rule (“if the email mentions ‘prize’, it's spam”). The teacher asks the first student to label the emails. Then the teacher circles the emails that student got wrong and tells the next student: “these circled ones count extra.” The second student picks the rule that does best on that re-weighted pile. Repeat.

At the end, the students vote — but students who were more accurate get a louder vote. Each student alone is barely better than guessing; together they're excellent.

How it works, step by step

  1. Start with equal weights on every training row: \(w_i = 1/n\). Labels are \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\).
  2. Fit a weak learner (a stump) that minimises the weighted error \(\varepsilon_m=\sum_{i:\,h_m(x_i)\ne y_i} w_i\).
  3. Give it a say: \(\alpha_m=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}\). Accurate learners get a large α; one at 50% error gets α = 0.
  4. Re-weight: \(w_i \leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha_m y_i h_m(x_i)}\) — mistakes multiplied by \(e^{\alpha}\), correct rows by \(e^{-\alpha}\) — then normalise to sum to 1.
  5. Repeat M times. Predict with the weighted vote \(\operatorname{sign}\big(\sum_m \alpha_m h_m(x)\big)\).
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“AdaBoost trains weak learners, usually stumps, sequentially. After each round it computes the weighted error ε, gives the learner a vote α = ½ ln((1 − ε)/ε), and multiplies the weights of misclassified rows by e^α so the next learner focuses on them. The final model is the sign of the α-weighted sum. It's equivalent to forward stagewise additive modelling with exponential loss, which is why it's powerful on clean data but sensitive to label noise and outliers — their weights grow exponentially. Gradient boosting generalises the idea to any differentiable loss.”

How it works — from layman to advanced

Level 1 · Plain English

Each round, ask “what is the team still getting wrong?”, make those examples matter more, and add a new simple rule that handles them. Trust each rule in proportion to how accurate it was on the data it was trained on.

Level 2 · Why those exact formulas

  • α grows with accuracy: ε = 0.1 → α = 1.10; ε = 0.3 → α = 0.42; ε = 0.5 → α = 0 (a coin flip gets no say). If ε > 0.5, α is negative — the learner's vote is flipped.
  • After re-weighting, the last learner looks like a coin flip. The misclassified rows end up holding exactly half of the total weight, so the next learner can't simply repeat the last one — it's forced to find something new.
  • Weak-learner condition: each learner only needs ε < 0.5 on its weighted data. Then training error falls exponentially: it's at most \(\prod_m 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_m(1-\varepsilon_m)}\le \exp\!\big(-2\sum_m\gamma_m^2\big)\), with \(\gamma_m = 0.5-\varepsilon_m\).

Level 4 · What advanced interviewers probe

∑ AdaBoost = forward stagewise modelling with exponential loss

Add one term at a time to \(F_{m}(x)=F_{m-1}(x)+\alpha h(x)\), choosing α and h to minimise the exponential loss \(L=\sum_i e^{-y_iF(x_i)}\). Writing \(w_i=e^{-y_iF_{m-1}(x_i)}\) (exactly AdaBoost's weights):

$$L(\alpha,h)=e^{-\alpha}\!\!\sum_{y_i=h(x_i)}\!\! w_i+e^{\alpha}\!\!\sum_{y_i\ne h(x_i)}\!\! w_i$$

For fixed α the best h minimises the weighted error; setting \(\partial L/\partial\alpha=0\) gives \(\alpha=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\). The population minimiser of exponential loss is \(F^*(x)=\tfrac12\ln\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=-1\mid x)}\) — half the log-odds — so probabilities can be recovered as \(\sigma(2F)\).

  • Margins: \(y_iF(x_i)/\sum\alpha\) is the confidence margin. AdaBoost keeps increasing margins after training error hits zero, which explains why test error often keeps falling — boosting's famous resistance to overfitting on clean data.
  • Noise sensitivity: exponential loss punishes a wrong, confident prediction as \(e^{|margin|}\). A mislabelled point keeps being misclassified, so its weight grows round after round and the model contorts itself around it. Logistic (deviance) loss grows only linearly — one reason gradient boosting with log-loss is more robust.
  • SAMME (multi-class): α = ln((1 − ε)/ε) + ln(K − 1), so a learner only needs to beat random guessing among K classes (ε < 1 − 1/K). sklearn uses SAMME (the probability-based SAMME.R was removed in 1.6).
  • AdaBoost.R2 (regression): re-weights by a scaled loss (linear, square or exponential) and combines by a weighted median.
  • Learning rate: multiplying α by ν < 1 (shrinkage) needs more rounds but usually generalises better.
  • Base learner depth: stumps give an additive model (no interactions); depth-2 or 3 trees let AdaBoost model interactions.

Must-know points for interviews

  • Sequential, re-weighting boosting: misclassified rows gain weight, correct rows lose it.
  • α = ½ ln((1 − ε)/ε); ε = 0.5 → α = 0; weighted-vote final prediction.
  • Minimises exponential loss via forward stagewise additive modelling.
  • Training error drops exponentially if each learner beats chance.
  • Sensitive to label noise and outliers (exponential weights).
  • Mainly reduces bias; base learners are stumps or shallow trees.
  • Gradient boosting generalises it to any differentiable loss by fitting residuals/gradients instead of re-weighting.

Pros & cons

Strengths

  • Few hyperparameters, simple to implement
  • Turns any weak learner into a strong one
  • Often resists overfitting on clean data (margins keep growing)
  • Built-in feature selection with stumps

Weaknesses

  • Sensitive to label noise and outliers
  • Sequential — can't parallelise across rounds
  • Usually less accurate than modern gradient boosting
  • Probabilities need calibration

Interview questions

How does AdaBoost work?

It trains weak learners sequentially on weighted data. Start with equal weights; each round fit a weak learner minimising weighted error ε, assign it α = ½ ln((1 − ε)/ε), multiply weights of misclassified rows by e^α and of correct rows by e^−α, normalise. Predict with sign(Σ αₘhₘ(x)).

What happens if a weak learner has error 0.5, or more than 0.5?

At ε = 0.5, α = 0: it gets no vote and weights don't change, so boosting stalls (sklearn stops early). At ε > 0.5, α is negative, which flips the learner's predictions — equivalent to a learner with error 1 − ε. In multi-class SAMME the threshold is 1 − 1/K.

What happens if a weak learner has zero error?

α → ∞ — the learner alone decides everything and boosting is done (implementations stop or clip ε to a small value). It usually means the base learner is too strong or there's leakage.

Why does AdaBoost focus on hard examples?

Weights are \(w_i\propto e^{-y_iF(x_i)}\): rows with negative margin (misclassified) grow exponentially. After each update, misclassified rows hold exactly half the weight, so the next learner must do better on them than the last one did.

Why is AdaBoost sensitive to outliers and label noise?

Exponential loss penalises a confidently wrong prediction as e^{margin}. A mislabelled point keeps being misclassified, its weight compounds, and later learners twist to fit it. Remedies: lower learning rate, fewer rounds, robust variants (LogitBoost, GentleBoost, gradient boosting with log or Huber loss), or cleaning labels.

Derive the AdaBoost α.

With weights \(w_i=e^{-y_iF_{m-1}(x_i)}\) normalised, the exponential loss of adding αh is \(e^{-\alpha}(1-\varepsilon)+e^{\alpha}\varepsilon\). Differentiate: \(-e^{-\alpha}(1-\varepsilon)+e^{\alpha}\varepsilon=0\Rightarrow e^{2\alpha}=(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\Rightarrow\alpha=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\).

What loss does AdaBoost minimise and what does its score estimate?

Exponential loss \(e^{-yF(x)}\), through forward stagewise additive modelling. Its population minimiser is half the log-odds, \(F^*(x)=\tfrac12\ln\frac{p}{1-p}\), so \(p=1/(1+e^{-2F})\). Exponential and log-loss share the minimiser but exponential loss punishes large negative margins far more.

Why does AdaBoost's test error often keep falling after training error reaches zero?

Margin theory (Schapire et al.): after every point is correct, further rounds still increase the margins \(y_iF(x_i)\), making the classifier more confident; generalisation bounds depend on the margin distribution rather than the number of rounds. It's not unlimited — with noise, AdaBoost eventually overfits.

AdaBoost vs gradient boosting?

AdaBoost changes row weights and keeps labels; it minimises exponential loss with a closed-form α. Gradient boosting keeps weights and changes targets — each tree fits the negative gradient of an arbitrary differentiable loss — so it handles regression, ranking, quantiles and robust losses. AdaBoost is gradient boosting with exponential loss (up to the weak-learner fitting step).

Why use stumps as the base learner?

They're weak (low variance, high bias), fast, and guarantee each round adds a small, simple correction. Stumps give an additive model with no interactions; if interactions matter, use depth-2 or 3 trees. Deep trees would overfit within a few rounds and defeat the purpose.

Can AdaBoost do regression or multi-class?

Yes: SAMME for multi-class (adds ln(K − 1) to α), AdaBoost.R2 for regression (re-weights by scaled absolute/squared/exponential error and combines by weighted median). In practice gradient boosting is preferred for both.

Where is AdaBoost used in practice?

Historically the Viola–Jones face detector (a cascade of boosted Haar-feature stumps). Today it's mainly a teaching tool and a quick baseline on clean, small data; gradient-boosting libraries have largely replaced it.
