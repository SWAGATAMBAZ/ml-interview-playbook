K-Means clustering

The go-to unsupervised algorithm. It groups similar points into K clusters with no labels needed. It's simple, fast and scales to millions of rows. Interviews test choosing K, initialisation, scaling and the shapes of data where it fails.

🧒 In plain English

You want to open 3 delivery hubs for customers spread across a city. Drop 3 pins on the map at random. Every customer goes to their nearest pin. Then move each pin to the centre of the crowd standing at it. Customers re-check which pin is now nearest, and the pins move again. Repeat until nobody switches. The pins have settled in the middle of 3 natural groups.

That's K-means: assign to nearest centre → move centre to the average → repeat.

How it works, step by step (no maths)

  1. Scale the features, because it's distance-based.
  2. Choose K, the number of clusters.
  3. Place K starting centres, spread out using k-means++.
  4. Assign every point to its nearest centre.
  5. Move each centre to the average of its points.
  6. Repeat 4–5 until assignments stop changing. Run it several times from different starts and keep the best (n_init).
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“K-means partitions data into K clusters by minimising the within-cluster sum of squared distances to each cluster's centroid. Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps: assign points to the nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean. Every step lowers the objective, so it always converges, but only to a local optimum, so I use k-means++ initialisation and several restarts. I choose K with the elbow method, silhouette score and business sense. It assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized clusters, needs scaled features, is sensitive to outliers, and every point is forced into some cluster. For odd shapes or noise I'd use DBSCAN, and for soft or elliptical clusters, a Gaussian Mixture Model.”

Must-know points for interviews

  • Objective: make points as close as possible to their cluster's centre (minimise inertia, i.e. the within-cluster sum of squares).
  • Always converges, but to a local optimum. The result depends on the starting centres, so use k-means++ and n_init restarts.
  • You must choose K: the elbow in the inertia curve, silhouette score (−1 to 1, higher is better), gap statistic, or business needs (“marketing can handle 5 segments”).
  • Scale features first, or the biggest-unit feature decides the clusters.
  • Assumes round, similar-size, similar-density clusters. It fails on rings, crescents, long thin shapes and very unequal sizes.
  • Sensitive to outliers, because the mean gets pulled. K-medoids or removing outliers first helps.
  • Hard assignment: every point belongs to exactly one cluster, even noise.
  • Fast: linear in the number of points. MiniBatchKMeans handles huge data.
  • Numeric features only, since means need numbers. Use K-modes or K-prototypes for categorical data.

Going deeper: advanced intuition

why it converges

Two moves, both downhill

Re-assigning a point to a closer centre can only lower the total distance, and moving a centre to its group's mean can only lower it too. The total can't fall forever, so the algorithm stops, though maybe in a valley that isn't the deepest one.

k-means++

Spread the starting pins

Each new starting centre is chosen with probability proportional to its squared distance from existing centres, so the centres start far apart. That gives faster convergence and much better results than purely random starts.

hidden assumption

Voronoi cells = straight borders

Cluster borders are always straight lines halfway between centres. That's why K-means can't separate a ring from its centre, or two crescent moons.

elbow is fuzzy

Inertia always drops with K

More clusters always means a lower inertia (K = n gives zero), so you look for the bend where improvements slow down. It's often ambiguous. Combine it with silhouette and domain sense.

GMM link

K-means is a special GMM

K-means equals a Gaussian Mixture Model with equal, round clusters and hard assignments. GMM relaxes both: elliptical shapes and soft probabilities.

high dimensions

Reduce first

In many dimensions distances lose contrast. Running PCA or using embeddings before K-means usually gives cleaner clusters.

When K-means wins (and when it doesn't)

🏆 Pick K-means when…
  • Customer segmentation on numeric behaviour (recency, frequency, spend), where you want a fixed number of actionable groups.
  • Data is large (millions of rows) and you need speed.
  • Clusters are roughly compact and blob-like.
  • Vector quantisation: compressing colours in an image, building a codebook, or pre-clustering embeddings for faster search.
  • You need a simple, explainable result: “cluster 2 = high-spend, infrequent buyers”.

⚠️ Avoid it when…
  • Clusters are non-convex (rings, moons), differ hugely in size or density, or there are many outliers.
  • Data is categorical or mixed.
  • You don't want every point forced into a cluster, e.g. for anomaly detection.
💼 Practical example to quote

Retail RFM segmentation: 2M customers described by recency, frequency and monetary value (log-transformed, then standardised). K-means with k-means++ and K = 5, chosen from the elbow plus silhouette plus the marketing team's capacity, produces segments like “champions”, “at-risk big spenders” and “one-time buyers”, each getting its own campaign.

Lloyd's algorithm

  1. Choose K and initialise K centroids (k-means++).
  2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat 2–3 until assignments stop changing (or centroid shift < tol / max_iter).

Complexity: O(n · K · d · iterations). Linear in n — scales to millions of points; MiniBatchKMeans for even larger data.

Step through it

Lloyd's algorithm, step by step
Initialise → Assign → Move. Try random init several times with K = 5 to land in a bad local minimum.

Initialisation: k-means++

Pick the first centroid uniformly at random; pick each next centroid with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\), the squared distance to the nearest already-chosen centroid. Spreads centroids out, gives an expected O(log K) approximation of the optimum, and converges faster. Still run several initialisations (n_init) and keep the lowest inertia.

Assumptions & when it fails

  • Spherical (isotropic), convex clusters of similar size and density — fails on elongated, ring/moon shapes or very unequal sizes.
  • Euclidean distance & scale — standardise features; dominated otherwise by large-range features.
  • Outliers pull means — use k-medoids, trimming, or remove outliers first.
  • Numeric features only — use k-modes / k-prototypes for categorical/mixed data.
  • K must be specified; every point is forced into a cluster (no noise label).
  • High dimensions: distances concentrate — reduce with PCA first.

Variants

  • Mini-batch K-means: updates centroids with small random batches — much faster, slightly worse inertia.
  • K-medoids (PAM): centres are actual data points; any distance; robust to outliers; O(n²).
  • K-modes / K-prototypes: categorical / mixed data.
  • Bisecting K-means: repeatedly split the largest cluster — hierarchical flavour.
  • Kernel / spectral clustering: non-convex shapes.

Objective & why it converges

∑ Within-cluster sum of squares (inertia)
$$J=\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{x_i\in C_k}\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2$$
  • Assignment step: for fixed centroids, assigning each point to its nearest centroid minimises J over assignments.
  • Update step: for fixed assignments, the mean minimises the sum of squared distances (set derivative to zero: \(\mu_k=\frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{x\in C_k}x\)).
  • J never increases and there are finitely many partitions ⇒ convergence in finite steps, to a local optimum. Finding the global optimum is NP-hard.
  • K-means is a special case of EM for a Gaussian mixture with equal, spherical covariances and hard assignments.

Interview questions

Explain the K-means algorithm.

Initialise K centroids; assign each point to the nearest centroid; recompute each centroid as the mean of its points; repeat until assignments stop changing. It minimises the within-cluster sum of squared distances (inertia).

Does K-means always converge? To the global optimum?

It always converges in a finite number of steps because both steps monotonically decrease (never increase) the objective and there are finitely many partitions. But only to a local minimum — results depend on initialisation, hence k-means++ and multiple restarts.

How do you choose K?

Elbow method on inertia, average silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin / Calinski–Harabasz indices, gap statistic, stability across bootstrap samples, and — often decisive — business interpretability/actionability of the segments.

What is k-means++ and why is it better?

An initialisation that picks the first centroid randomly and each subsequent one with probability ∝ squared distance to the nearest chosen centroid. It spreads centroids out, avoids several centroids in one cluster, gives an O(log K) approximation guarantee in expectation, and speeds convergence.

What are the assumptions/limitations of K-means?

Clusters are spherical, convex, similar in size and density; Euclidean geometry; features on the same scale; K known; sensitive to outliers and initialisation; numeric data only; every point assigned (no noise). Fails on moons/rings, elongated clusters, varied densities.

Why scale features before K-means?

Euclidean distances are dominated by large-range features, so clusters would be formed almost entirely along those. Standardise (or use domain-meaningful weights) so each feature contributes appropriately.

What is the silhouette score?

For each point: s = (b − a)/max(a, b), where a is its mean distance to points in its own cluster and b its mean distance to points of the nearest other cluster. Ranges −1 to 1: near 1 well clustered, ~0 on a boundary, negative probably misassigned. Average over points to compare K.

How is K-means related to Gaussian Mixture Models?

K-means is the limit of EM for a GMM with equal, spherical covariances (σ²I, σ → 0) and equal weights, with hard instead of soft assignments. GMMs generalise it: elliptical clusters, different sizes, soft probabilistic memberships.

How would you cluster categorical data?

K-modes (matching dissimilarity, modes as centres), K-prototypes for mixed data, hierarchical clustering with Gower distance, or embed categories (e.g. entity embeddings / MCA) and then cluster.

How do you evaluate clustering without labels — and with labels?

Without: internal metrics (silhouette, DB, CH), stability across resamples, and qualitative/business review of segment profiles. With ground truth: Adjusted Rand Index, Normalised/Adjusted Mutual Information, homogeneity/completeness/V-measure.

What's the complexity of K-means and how do you scale it?

O(n·K·d·iterations) time, O((n + K)·d) memory. Scale with MiniBatchKMeans, Elkan's algorithm (triangle inequality), PCA to reduce d, sampling for initialisation, or distributed implementations (Spark MLlib).

Give a business use case and how you'd validate the result.

Customer segmentation on RFM (recency, frequency, monetary) features: log-transform and scale, choose K with silhouette + marketing input, profile clusters (means, sizes), name them (“lapsed high-value”), check stability over time, and validate by running targeted campaigns (A/B tests) per segment.
