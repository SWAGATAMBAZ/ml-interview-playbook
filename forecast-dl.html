🧒 In plain English

A recurrent network reads a series one step at a time, like you reading a sentence, keeping a short memory note that it updates at every step. Plain RNNs have a leaky memory: after 20–30 steps, the early information has faded. An LSTM adds a separate long-term memory “conveyor belt” with three valves (gates): forget (erase old info), input (write new info) and output (decide what to reveal now). Because information can ride the conveyor belt untouched, it remembers much longer.

Newer models skip the step-by-step reading: N-BEATS is a deep stack of simple fully-connected blocks, transformers look at all past steps at once with attention, and foundation models are pretrained on billions of time points so they can forecast a new series without any training (“zero-shot”), like a language model completing a sentence.

🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“For deep forecasting I window the series into lookback/horizon pairs, normalise each window or series, and train a global model across many series. LSTMs solve the vanishing-gradient problem of RNNs with an additive cell state controlled by forget, input and output gates. In practice I'd consider DeepAR for probabilistic forecasts over many related series, N-BEATS/N-HiTS for strong univariate accuracy, TFT when there are many known-future and static covariates and interpretability matters, or a pretrained foundation model like Chronos or TimesFM as a zero-shot baseline. Deep models pay off with lots of related series, long histories and rich covariates; on a few short series, ETS or LightGBM usually win, and I'd always benchmark against seasonal naive.”

Data preparation for neural forecasters

Windowing a series into training samples
Each sample is (last L values → next H values). Neural nets are trained on many such windows from many series.
  • Windowing: lookback L (context) is a key hyperparameter — often 2–7× the horizon or several seasonal cycles.
  • Scaling is essential: neural nets need inputs on similar scales. Per-series standardisation or per-window normalisation (subtract the window mean, divide by its std, add back at the output) — formalised as RevIN (reversible instance normalisation) — also handles trend and level shifts.
  • Covariates: separate past-observed, known-future and static inputs; models like TFT and DeepAR treat them differently.
  • Splits: time-based train/validation/test with no window overlapping the boundary.
  • Sampling: sample windows across series (weight by importance or volume) instead of iterating series by series.

Recurrent neural networks and vanishing gradients

∑ A vanilla RNN
$$h_t=\tanh(W_hh_{t-1}+W_xx_t+b),\qquad \hat y_t=W_yh_t$$

Training uses backpropagation through time (BPTT). The gradient of a loss at time T with respect to an early hidden state is a product of Jacobians: \(\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k}=\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\tanh')\,W_h\). If the largest singular value is below 1, it shrinks exponentially (vanishing — can't learn long dependencies); above 1, it explodes (fix with gradient clipping).

LSTM: gates and an additive memory

∑ The LSTM cell (σ = sigmoid, ⊙ = element-wise product)
$$\begin{aligned} f_t&=\sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f) &&\text{forget gate: how much old memory to keep}\\ i_t&=\sigma(W_i[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_i) &&\text{input gate: how much new info to write}\\ \tilde c_t&=\tanh(W_c[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_c) &&\text{candidate memory}\\ c_t&=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t &&\text{cell state: additive update}\\ o_t&=\sigma(W_o[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_o) &&\text{output gate}\\ h_t&=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t) &&\text{hidden state / output} \end{aligned}$$

Why it fixes vanishing gradients: \(\partial c_t/\partial c_{t-1}=f_t\) (plus small indirect terms). When the forget gate is near 1, gradients flow back through the cell state almost unchanged — the “constant error carousel”. Initialising the forget-gate bias to 1 or more is a standard trick.

Parameters: each of the 4 gates has a weight matrix of size k × (k + d) plus bias, so an LSTM layer has \(4(k(k+d)+k)\) parameters for hidden size k and input size d (e.g. k = 64, d = 1 → 16,896).

GRU: a lighter LSTM

The Gated Recurrent Unit merges the cell and hidden states and uses two gates: an update gate z (how much to replace the state, like forget + input combined) and a reset gate r (how much past state to use in the candidate). \(h_t=(1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\tilde h_t\). About 25% fewer parameters than an LSTM, often similar accuracy; a good default when data are limited.

Sequence-to-sequence, teacher forcing and multi-step output

  • Encoder–decoder: an encoder RNN reads the lookback window into a state; a decoder RNN generates the horizon step by step, optionally fed known-future covariates.
  • Teacher forcing: during training the decoder receives the true previous value; at inference it receives its own prediction — a train/test mismatch (exposure bias). Mitigate with scheduled sampling, or avoid it with a direct multi-output head.
  • Multi-output (MIMO) head: a dense layer outputs all H steps at once — simple and often best.
  • Attention lets the decoder look back at all encoder states instead of one bottleneck vector.

Convolutional models: TCN and WaveNet

Temporal Convolutional Networks use causal convolutions (output at t sees only ≤ t) with dilations 1, 2, 4, 8… so the receptive field grows exponentially with depth. They train in parallel (no step-by-step recurrence), have stable gradients, and often match LSTMs. WaveNet is the audio ancestor.

Transformers and the DLinear critique

The 2023 paper “Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?” showed that a one-layer linear model on decomposed series (DLinear) beat several sophisticated transformers on standard long-horizon benchmarks. The reasons are worth stating in an interview:

  • Self-attention is permutation-invariant; positional encodings only partially restore order, yet order is the whole point in time series.
  • Many benchmarks are dominated by trend and seasonality that linear models capture well; point-wise tokens carry little semantic information.
  • Over-parameterised models overfit small, non-stationary datasets.

The field responded with patching (PatchTST), channel independence, instance normalisation and inverted tokens — and with much better baselines. Lesson: always include a linear and a seasonal-naive baseline.

Pretrained on huge corpora of real and synthetic series, they forecast new series zero-shot (no training) or after light fine-tuning.

The interview-winning answer to “ARIMA or LSTM?”: “It depends on the data volume and structure. For a single series with a few hundred points, ARIMA/ETS — an LSTM will overfit and won't beat it. For thousands of related series with covariates, a global model: I'd benchmark LightGBM first, then a deep model like DeepAR or TFT if it adds value in backtests. I'd decide on rolling-origin error per horizon and on operational cost, not on novelty.”

Training tips that matter

  • Normalise per series or per window (RevIN); predict in normalised space and invert.
  • Use a global model across series; sample windows; add series embeddings for IDs.
  • Gradient clipping for RNNs; forget-gate bias ≈ 1; Adam with LR schedules; early stopping on a time-based validation set.
  • Dropout (including recurrent dropout), weight decay, and ensembling over seeds/lookbacks — N-BEATS ensembles of 180 models were key to its M4 results.
  • Choose the loss to match the decision: quantile loss for intervals, distribution heads (DeepAR) for sample paths.
  • Libraries: neuralforecast (Nixtla), darts, pytorch-forecasting, GluonTS, autogluon.timeseries.

Must-know points for interviews

  • RNN: hidden state recurrence; BPTT; vanishing/exploding gradients.
  • LSTM: forget, input, output gates + additive cell state → long memory; 4× the parameters of an RNN.
  • GRU: update + reset gates; fewer parameters.
  • Normalise windows/series; train global models; use direct multi-output heads.
  • DeepAR = probabilistic global RNN; N-BEATS/N-HiTS = MLP stacks; TFT = covariates + interpretability; PatchTST = patched transformer.
  • DLinear critique: simple linear baselines beat many transformers.
  • Foundation models: zero-shot TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai; strong at short horizons; benchmark yourself.
  • DL wins with scale (many related series, long history, rich covariates); otherwise ETS/LightGBM.

Interview questions

Why use an LSTM instead of a vanilla RNN?

Vanilla RNN gradients vanish (or explode) exponentially over time steps because of repeated multiplication by the recurrent Jacobian, so they can't learn long-range dependencies. LSTMs add a cell state updated additively and controlled by gates; with the forget gate near 1, gradients pass through many steps nearly unchanged.

Explain each LSTM gate.

Forget gate f (sigmoid): fraction of the previous cell state to keep. Input gate i (sigmoid): how much of the new candidate (tanh of input and previous hidden state) to write. Output gate o (sigmoid): how much of tanh(cell state) to expose as the hidden state. Cell update: c = f⊙c₋₁ + i⊙c̃; output h = o⊙tanh(c).

LSTM vs GRU?

GRU merges cell and hidden state and uses update and reset gates — fewer parameters (3 gate blocks vs 4), faster, often similar accuracy, better with less data. LSTM's separate memory and output gate give a bit more control for very long dependencies. Try both; differences are usually small compared with data preparation choices.

How do you prepare data for an LSTM forecaster?

Window the series into (lookback, horizon) pairs; scale per series or per window (and invert); encode covariates as extra input channels, separating past-only from known-future; split by time without overlapping windows across boundaries; batch windows sampled across many series; shape inputs as (batch, time, features).

What is teacher forcing and its downside?

Feeding the true previous target into the decoder during training. It speeds and stabilises training, but at inference the model sees its own (imperfect) predictions, a distribution shift called exposure bias that makes errors compound. Remedies: scheduled sampling, training on multi-step losses, or direct multi-output heads.

How does DeepAR produce probabilistic forecasts?

A global autoregressive RNN trained on many series; at each step it outputs parameters of a chosen likelihood (Gaussian, negative binomial for counts, Student-t) and is trained by maximising likelihood. At inference it samples a value, feeds it back, and repeats to generate many sample paths; quantiles of the paths give intervals. Series are scaled by their mean and covariates/embeddings enable cross-learning and cold start.

What is N-BEATS?

A deep stack of fully connected blocks with doubly residual connections: each block outputs a backcast (subtracted from its input so later blocks model what's left) and a partial forecast (summed across blocks). A generic version learns free bases; an interpretable version constrains blocks to polynomial trend and Fourier seasonality. It won top results on M4 without any time-series-specific components; N-HiTS adds multi-rate sampling for long horizons.

What makes the Temporal Fusion Transformer suitable for business forecasting?

It distinguishes static, past-observed and known-future inputs; uses variable-selection networks and gating to suppress irrelevant inputs; an LSTM for local patterns and interpretable multi-head attention for long-range ones; and outputs multiple quantiles. It provides variable importances and attention patterns that help explain forecasts to stakeholders.

Why did simple linear models beat transformers on long-horizon benchmarks?

Attention is permutation-invariant and point-wise tokens carry little information, so transformers struggled to exploit temporal order; the datasets are dominated by trend and seasonality that a linear map captures; and large models overfit non-stationary, modest-sized data. Patching, channel independence and instance normalisation (PatchTST) recovered transformer performance. The lesson is to include strong simple baselines.

What are time-series foundation models and when would you use one?

Large models (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai and others) pretrained on billions of time points so they can forecast unseen series zero-shot. Use them as a fast strong baseline, for cold start or many heterogeneous series with little time to engineer features, or as an ensemble member. Validate on your own backtests — they're strongest at short horizons, may not use your domain covariates well, and inference cost/latency matters at scale.

ARIMA or LSTM for a forecasting problem?

Depends on the data. A single or few series with hundreds of points and mostly linear autocorrelation: ARIMA/ETS — interpretable, fast, an LSTM would overfit. Thousands of related series, long histories, nonlinear effects and covariates: a global model — benchmark LightGBM, then deep models like DeepAR/TFT. Decide with rolling-origin backtests per horizon against seasonal naive, plus cost and maintainability.

Why normalise each window, and what is RevIN?

Series and windows differ in level and scale and drift over time (non-stationarity); networks generalise poorly to input ranges they haven't seen. Subtracting the window mean (and dividing by its std), predicting in that space and adding the statistics back makes the model level-invariant. RevIN formalises this as reversible instance normalisation with learnable affine parameters.

How many parameters does an LSTM layer have?

4 × (k × (k + d) + k) for hidden size k and input size d — four gate blocks each with input weights, recurrent weights and a bias. For k = 64, d = 10: 4 × (64 × 74 + 64) = 19,200. (PyTorch keeps two bias vectors per gate, so it reports 4 × (k(k + d) + 2k).)
