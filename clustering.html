Hierarchical, DBSCAN, GMM & anomaly detection

Beyond K-means: four tools for finding groups and oddities in unlabelled data. Hierarchical clustering builds a family tree of groups, DBSCAN finds dense crowds of any shape, Gaussian Mixtures give soft, probabilistic clusters, and anomaly detectors flag the points that fit nowhere. Each model below gets its own plain-English explanation and a “when it wins” box.

🧒 In plain English

Imagine a satellite photo of people in a park at night. Hierarchical clustering starts with every person alone, keeps merging the two closest groups, and records the full family tree. DBSCAN says “a cluster is wherever people are packed tightly together”, so crowds can be any shape and the loners are noise. GMM assumes each crowd is a fuzzy oval and says “this person is 70% crowd A, 30% crowd B”. Anomaly detection ignores the crowds and finds the people who are strangely far from everyone.

🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“I choose the clustering method by the shape of the clusters and what I need out of them. K-means is for fast, round clusters when I know K. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering gives a dendrogram, so there's no need to fix K upfront, and the linkage choice controls cluster shape, but it's O(n²) in memory, so it suits smaller data. DBSCAN groups density-connected points, finds arbitrary shapes and labels outliers as noise. Its weakness is one global density, which HDBSCAN fixes. GMMs fit a mixture of Gaussians with EM, giving soft assignments and elliptical clusters, with K chosen by BIC. For anomalies I'd use Isolation Forest for scale, LOF for local density outliers, or reconstruction error from an autoencoder or PCA.”

Each model in plain English

1 · Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

🧒 Idea

Start with every point as its own group. Repeatedly merge the two closest groups until one remains. The merge history is a tree (dendrogram), and you cut it at whatever height gives a sensible number of groups.

  • No need to pick K first. Look at the dendrogram, then decide.
  • Linkage = how “distance between groups” is defined. Ward (the most common) gives compact, K-means-like groups. Single chains into long shapes and is noise-sensitive. Complete gives tight groups. Average is in between.
  • Expensive: memory grows with n², so it's practical up to roughly 10–20k points.
  • Deterministic (no random start), but merges are greedy and can't be undone.
🏆 Wins when…

Data is small to medium and you want a taxonomy or nested structure: product categories, gene-expression families, document topics within topics, or an org-chart-like view of customer types. It's also good when stakeholders want to see how groups relate.

2 · DBSCAN (and HDBSCAN)

🧒 Idea

A point with at least min_samples neighbours within radius eps is a core point, sitting inside a crowd. Clusters grow by chaining core points together. Points near a crowd join it, and points near nobody are labelled noise.

  • Finds arbitrary shapes (rings, moons, rivers) and doesn't need K.
  • Built-in outlier detection: noise points get the label −1.
  • Choose eps from a k-distance plot (the “knee”). Set min_samples to roughly 2 × the number of dimensions.
  • Weakness: one global density. It fails when clusters have different densities. HDBSCAN fixes this and needs only a minimum cluster size.
  • It struggles in high dimensions, because distances blur. Reduce dimensions first.
🏆 Wins when…

Geospatial or GPS data (finding hotspots, delivery zones, ride pick-up clusters), irregularly shaped clusters, unknown K, and data with noise you want flagged rather than forced into a cluster. Prefer HDBSCAN when densities vary.

3 · Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

🧒 Idea

Assume the data was produced by K overlapping fuzzy ovals (bell curves). The EM algorithm alternates two steps: guess how much each point belongs to each oval, then re-fit the ovals to those soft memberships. The output is a probability per cluster for every point.

  • Soft clustering: “70% segment A, 30% segment B”, which is useful when groups overlap.
  • Elliptical clusters with different sizes and orientations. K-means is the special case with round, equal clusters and hard assignments.
  • Choose K with BIC or AIC, since it's a probabilistic model.
  • It's a density model too: a very low likelihood flags an anomaly, and you can sample new data from it.
  • Sensitive to initialisation (local optima). Use the full covariance only with enough data per cluster.
🏆 Wins when…

Clusters overlap or are elongated, you need membership probabilities (e.g. a customer partly in two personas), or you need a density estimate for anomaly scoring or simulation.

4 · Anomaly detection (Isolation Forest, LOF and others)

🧒 Idea

Isolation Forest: keep cutting the data with random lines. Normal points are buried in crowds and take many cuts to isolate, while oddballs get isolated in just a few. LOF: compare how crowded a point's neighbourhood is with how crowded its neighbours' neighbourhoods are. A point much lonelier than its neighbours is an outlier.

  • Isolation Forest: fast, scales to millions of rows, copes with many features. contamination sets the expected share of outliers.
  • LOF: catches local outliers in data with varying density, but it's slower.
  • Simple rules (z-score, IQR) are fine for one variable. Reconstruction error (PCA or an autoencoder) works for complex, high-dimensional data.
  • Labels are usually rare, so evaluate on whatever labelled incidents exist (precision@k) and tune the threshold to the team's investigation capacity.
🏆 Wins when…

Fraud, intrusion, sensor faults or quality defects with few or no labels. Start with Isolation Forest at scale, use LOF for local, density-varying outliers, and switch to a supervised classifier once enough confirmed anomalies exist.

⚠️ Traps that apply to all of them
  • Scale the features. Every method here is distance- or density-based.
  • Clustering always returns something, even on random data. Validate with silhouette scores, stability across runs and, above all, whether the groups are actionable for the business.
  • In high dimensions, reduce first (PCA, UMAP) or cluster embeddings.
💼 Practical example to quote

Ride-hailing pick-up hotspots: 5M GPS pick-up points across a city. K-means would slice the city into round blobs and force stray points into them. HDBSCAN finds natural hotspots of any shape (stations, malls, along a main road) at different densities and labels scattered pick-ups as noise. Isolation Forest on trip features flags suspicious trips separately.

Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

  1. Start with n singleton clusters.
  2. Merge the two closest clusters according to the linkage criterion.
  3. Repeat until one cluster remains; record merges in a dendrogram.
  4. Cut the dendrogram at a height (distance threshold) or at K clusters.
LinkageDistance between clustersBehaviour
SingleClosest pair of pointsFinds elongated shapes; “chaining” effect, sensitive to noise
CompleteFarthest pair of pointsCompact, similar-diameter clusters; outlier-sensitive
Average (UPGMA)Mean of all pairwise distancesCompromise
WardIncrease in total within-cluster variance after mergingCompact, similar-size, K-means-like; Euclidean only; usual default

Pros: no K upfront, dendrogram reveals structure at all scales, any distance (except Ward). Cons: O(n²) memory, O(n² log n)–O(n³) time, greedy merges can't be undone. Divisive (top-down) clustering is the reverse.

DBSCAN

  • Core point: has ≥ min_samples points (including itself) within radius eps.
  • Border point: within eps of a core point but not core itself.
  • Noise: neither — labelled −1.
  • Clusters = maximal sets of density-connected points (chains of core points + their borders).

Choosing eps: k-distance plot — sort each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (k = min_samples) and pick the “knee”. min_samples: rule of thumb ≥ d + 1, often 2·d. Pros: arbitrary shapes, no K, explicit outliers, robust to noise. Cons: single global density (fails when densities vary — use HDBSCAN), sensitive to eps, struggles in high dimensions, needs scaling.

Gaussian Mixture Models & EM

∑ Mixture density
$$p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k),\qquad\sum_k\pi_k=1$$

E-step: responsibilities \(\gamma_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}\) (soft assignments).
M-step: \(N_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\), \(\pi_k=N_k/n\), \(\mu_k=\frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i\), \(\Sigma_k=\frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)^T\).
Each EM iteration never decreases the log-likelihood; converges to a local maximum.

  • covariance_type: full (any ellipse), tied (shared), diag (axis-aligned), spherical (K-means-like).
  • Choose K with BIC/AIC (penalised likelihood) — lower is better.
  • Gives probabilities, densities (for anomaly scores: low p(x) = unusual), and can generate samples.
  • Risks: singular covariances (a component collapses onto a point) — use reg_covar; local optima — n_init.

Interview questions

How does hierarchical clustering work and what's a dendrogram?

Agglomerative: start with each point as a cluster and repeatedly merge the two closest clusters per the linkage criterion until one remains. The dendrogram is the tree of merges with heights = merge distances; cutting it horizontally gives a flat clustering, and long vertical gaps suggest natural cluster counts.

Compare single, complete, average and Ward linkage.

Single: min pairwise distance — follows chains, finds non-convex shapes but sensitive to noise. Complete: max distance — compact clusters, outlier-sensitive. Average: mean distance — balanced. Ward: minimises the increase in within-cluster variance — compact, equal-size clusters similar to K-means; requires Euclidean distance.

Explain DBSCAN and its parameters.

Density-based clustering: a core point has at least min_samples points within distance eps; clusters grow by connecting core points that are within eps of each other plus their border points; everything else is noise. eps sets the neighbourhood size, min_samples the density threshold. Choose eps from the knee of a k-distance plot.

When does DBSCAN fail?

Clusters with very different densities (one eps can't fit all — use HDBSCAN or OPTICS), high-dimensional data where distances concentrate, unscaled features, and when eps is poorly chosen (too small → everything noise, too large → one cluster).

K-means vs GMM?

K-means: hard assignments, spherical equal-variance clusters, minimises inertia, faster. GMM: soft probabilistic assignments, elliptical clusters with their own covariances and weights, maximises likelihood via EM, gives densities and supports BIC model selection. K-means is a limiting case of GMM.

Explain the EM algorithm.

For models with latent variables: the E-step computes the posterior over latent variables (responsibilities) given current parameters; the M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood to update parameters. Repeat until convergence. Log-likelihood never decreases; converges to a local optimum, so use multiple inits.

How does Isolation Forest detect anomalies?

It builds random trees by picking a random feature and a random split value between its min and max. Anomalies are few and different, so they get isolated near the root — short average path lengths across trees. The anomaly score normalises the average path length by that expected for a random point (c(n)).

What is Local Outlier Factor?

LOF compares a point's local reachability density to that of its k nearest neighbours. Points in a region much sparser than their neighbours' regions get LOF ≫ 1. It finds local outliers in datasets with clusters of different densities, unlike global distance rules.

How would you build an anomaly detection system for transactions without labels?

Engineer behavioural features (amount vs user's history, velocity, new device/merchant, time-of-day), scale, fit an unsupervised detector (Isolation Forest / LOF / autoencoder), set the threshold by alert capacity (top-k per day), have analysts review alerts, and use their feedback as labels to move to a supervised model (with the unsupervised score as a feature). Monitor drift and alert precision.

How do you choose the number of components in a GMM?

Fit over a range of K (and covariance types) and pick the lowest BIC = −2 log L + p log n (AIC uses 2p, tends to choose more components). Also check stability, cluster sizes and interpretability; a Bayesian GMM with a Dirichlet process prior can shrink unused components automatically.

Difference between outlier detection and novelty detection?

Outlier detection: training data contain outliers; find them in that data (unsupervised). Novelty detection: train on clean normal data only, then flag new observations that differ (semi-supervised), e.g. One-Class SVM or LOF(novelty=True).
