Statistics & A/B testing

Data scientist loops almost always include a statistics/experimentation round: distributions, CLT, hypothesis tests, p-values, confidence intervals, Type I/II errors, power and sample size, and designing and reading A/B tests — including the pitfalls (peeking, multiple testing, SRM, novelty effects).

Descriptive statistics

  • Centre: mean (outlier-sensitive), median (robust), mode.
  • Spread: variance, standard deviation, IQR, MAD. Sample variance divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction: unbiased because we estimated the mean).
  • Shape: skewness (right-skewed: mean > median, e.g. income), kurtosis (tail heaviness).
  • Relationship: covariance, Pearson correlation (linear), Spearman (rank/monotonic), Kendall's τ. Correlation ≠ causation; watch confounders and Simpson's paradox.

Central Limit Theorem & law of large numbers

LLN: the sample mean converges to the population mean as n grows. CLT: for i.i.d. samples with finite variance, the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, \(\bar X\approx\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\), regardless of the population's shape (n ≳ 30 rule of thumb; more for heavy skew). Standard error \(=\sigma/\sqrt n\). This is why z/t-tests and normal-based CIs work on non-normal metrics like revenue per user (with enough data).

Hypothesis testing

  1. State H₀ (no effect) and H₁ (effect; one- or two-sided).
  2. Choose α (significance level, usually 0.05) and the test.
  3. Compute the test statistic from data, e.g. \(z=\frac{\hat p_B-\hat p_A}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)(1/n_A+1/n_B)}}\).
  4. p-value = P(statistic at least this extreme | H₀ true).
  5. Reject H₀ if p < α; report effect size and CI, not just significance.

⚠️ What a p-value is NOT

Not the probability that H₀ is true, not the probability the result is due to chance, not the size or importance of the effect. “Fail to reject” ≠ “accept H₀” (absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence — maybe the test was underpowered).

Confidence intervals

A 95% CI: if we repeated the experiment many times, 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true parameter. (Not “95% probability the true value is in this interval” — that's a Bayesian credible interval.) For a mean: \(\bar x\pm t_{0.975,n-1}\,s/\sqrt n\); for a proportion: \(\hat p\pm1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). A CI excluding 0 for a difference ⇔ p < 0.05 (two-sided). Bootstrap CIs for anything without a formula.

Power and sample size

Power = P(detect an effect | it truly exists), conventionally 80%. It increases with sample size, effect size and α, and decreases with variance. For two proportions:

∑ Sample size per group (approx.)
$$n\approx\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2\,\big[p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)\big]}{(p_2-p_1)^2}\qquad\text{rule of thumb: } n\approx\frac{16\,\sigma^2}{\delta^2}$$

Designing an A/B test (interview template)

  1. Goal & hypothesis: “New checkout flow increases purchase conversion.”
  2. Metrics: primary (conversion), secondary (revenue/user, AOV), guardrails (latency, refunds, unsubscribes).
  3. Unit of randomisation: user (not session/page view) to avoid inconsistent experience; cluster randomisation when there's interference (marketplaces, social networks).
  4. Sample size & duration: from baseline, MDE, α, power; run whole weeks (weekly seasonality), usually ≥ 1–2 weeks.
  5. Launch checks: A/A test, sample ratio mismatch (SRM) χ² check, logging sanity.
  6. Analysis: pre-registered test, effect size + CI, segment analysis as exploratory, variance reduction (CUPED).
  7. Decision: statistical + practical significance, guardrails OK, cost of change; ramp up gradually.

Experimentation pitfalls

  • Peeking / early stopping inflates false positives — fix n in advance or use sequential tests (alpha spending, mSPRT)
  • Multiple comparisons (many metrics/variants/segments) — Bonferroni (α/m) or Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR)
  • Sample ratio mismatch — assignment/logging bug; results untrustworthy
  • Novelty & primacy effects — run longer, look at cohorts over time
  • Network effects / interference — cluster or switchback designs
  • Simpson's paradox — aggregated vs segmented results disagree due to mix shifts
  • Underpowered tests — “no significant difference” is inconclusive
  • Heavy-tailed metrics (revenue) — winsorise, log, bootstrap, CUPED
  • Ratio metrics (CTR per session) — delta method or bootstrap; unit mismatch with randomisation unit

Probability & Bayes quick hits

  • Bayes: \(P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}\). Classic: a 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-1000 disease → P(disease | positive) ≈ 9% (base-rate fallacy).
  • Independence: P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B). Conditional independence underpins Naive Bayes.
  • Expected value & variance rules: Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X); Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2Cov(X, Y).
  • Bayesian A/B testing: Beta priors on conversion rates → posterior P(B > A), expected loss; intuitive but needs prior choices.

Interview questions

Explain a p-value to a non-technical person.

“If the new design truly made no difference, how surprising would results like ours be?” A p-value of 0.01 means results this extreme would happen only 1% of the time by random chance alone — so we doubt the “no difference” assumption. It doesn't tell us how big or important the effect is.

What is the Central Limit Theorem and why does it matter?

The distribution of the sample mean of i.i.d. variables with finite variance approaches a normal distribution as n grows, with standard error σ/√n, regardless of the original distribution. It justifies normal-based CIs and z/t-tests on averages of non-normal metrics in A/B tests.

Type I vs Type II error — which is worse?

Type I: false positive (ship a change that doesn't work), controlled by α. Type II: false negative (miss a real improvement), rate β; power = 1 − β. Which is worse depends on costs: in drug approval Type I is worse; in early-stage product exploration a Type II error (killing a good idea) may cost more.

How do you determine the sample size for an A/B test?

Specify baseline metric value and variance, the minimum detectable effect worth acting on, α (0.05) and power (0.8); use the power formula (n ≈ 16σ²/δ² per group for α=0.05, power 0.8) or a calculator. Convert to duration with traffic, rounding up to full weeks.

What is statistical power and how can you increase it?

The probability of detecting a true effect of a given size. Increase it with more samples, larger effects (bolder changes), lower variance (CUPED/covariate adjustment, better metrics, stratification, trimming outliers), higher α, or one-sided tests when justified.

Why is peeking at A/B test results a problem?

Each look is another chance to cross the significance threshold by luck, so stopping as soon as p < 0.05 inflates the false-positive rate far above 5%. Pre-commit to a sample size, or use sequential methods (group sequential designs with alpha spending, always-valid p-values).

How do you handle multiple testing?

Pre-specify one primary metric; correct for many comparisons with Bonferroni (α/m, conservative, controls family-wise error) or Benjamini–Hochberg (controls false discovery rate, more power). Treat segment findings as hypotheses for follow-up tests.

What is sample ratio mismatch?

When the observed split of users between variants differs significantly from the designed split (e.g. 50.8/49.2 with millions of users, χ² p < 0.001). It signals bugs in randomisation, assignment, redirects or logging, and invalidates the results until fixed.

What would you do if your A/B test is significant for one metric but a guardrail metric got worse?

Quantify both effects with CIs and translate to business value; check whether the guardrail drop is real (SRM, novelty, segments); discuss the trade-off with stakeholders; consider iterating on the design to keep the gain without the harm; don't ship blindly on the primary metric.

What is Simpson's paradox?

A trend in aggregated data reverses within every subgroup because of a confounding variable that changes the mix (e.g. treatment looks worse overall but better within each severity level because sicker patients got it more often). Look at stratified results and think causally.

t-test vs z-test vs chi-square test?

z-test: means/proportions with known variance or large samples. t-test: means with estimated variance (small/moderate n); Welch's version for unequal variances. χ² test: categorical counts — goodness of fit or independence (e.g. conversion vs variant), equivalent to the two-proportion z-test for 2×2 tables.

What is CUPED?

Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data: adjust the metric with a pre-period covariate, Y' = Y − θ(X − mean X), θ = Cov(X, Y)/Var(X). It keeps the estimate unbiased (randomisation makes X balanced) while reducing variance by a factor (1 − ρ²), shortening experiments.

What is a confidence interval? Interpret a 95% CI of [1.2%, 3.5%] lift.

The procedure produces intervals that contain the true lift 95% of the time across repeated experiments. Here: the data are consistent with a true lift between 1.2% and 3.5%; since 0 is excluded the effect is significant at α=0.05, and even the low end may or may not clear the practical-significance bar.

A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity for a disease with 0.1% prevalence — P(disease | positive)?

Per 100,000 people: 100 sick → 99 true positives; 99,900 healthy → 999 false positives. P = 99 / (99 + 999) ≈ 9%. The base rate dominates — the base-rate fallacy.

When can't you run an A/B test, and what then?

Ethical/legal constraints, network effects you can't isolate, one-off launches, too little traffic, or long-term outcomes. Use quasi-experiments: difference-in-differences, synthetic control, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables, propensity score matching, interrupted time series — with careful assumption checks.
