Time-series forecasting

Predicting the future from the past: sales, demand, traffic, prices. Interviews care less about formulas and more about whether you respect time: stationarity, no shuffling, proper backtesting and picking the right family of models. 🧒 In plain English

To forecast tomorrow's ice-cream sales, you'd look at: the trend (sales growing year on year), seasonality (more in summer, more on weekends), recent momentum (yesterday was unusually high) and outside drivers (a heatwave, a promotion). Every forecasting model is a different way of combining these four ingredients.

The golden rule: never let the model peek into the future. Train on the past, test on what came after, just as it'll be used in real life.

🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“I start by decomposing the series into trend, seasonality and residual, and checking stationarity with ADF or KPSS, differencing or log-transforming as needed. For a few series with clear structure I use classical models: ETS or Holt-Winters for trend plus seasonality, and SARIMA or SARIMAX when autocorrelation and external regressors matter, picking orders from ACF/PACF plots and AIC. For many related series with rich drivers I build a global gradient-boosting model on lag, rolling and calendar features, making sure every feature only uses past information. I validate with rolling-origin (walk-forward) backtests, never random K-fold, compare against naive and seasonal-naive baselines, and report MAE, RMSE or MASE, plus prediction intervals.”

Must-know points for interviews

  • Components: trend, seasonality, cycles and noise. Use an additive model when the seasonal swing is constant, and a multiplicative one when it grows with the level (log the series to make it additive).
  • Stationarity means a constant mean, variance and autocorrelation over time. ARIMA needs it, so difference to remove trend and seasonal-difference to remove seasonality. ADF: p < 0.05 means stationary. KPSS: the opposite.
  • ACF/PACF plots help pick ARIMA orders. A PACF that cuts off at lag p suggests AR(p). An ACF that cuts off at lag q suggests MA(q).
  • Never shuffle. Use time-based splits and walk-forward or expanding-window backtesting (TimeSeriesSplit).
  • Leakage traps: rolling means that include today, features unknown at forecast time (future prices), or scaling with full-period statistics.
  • Always beat a baseline: naive (tomorrow = today) and seasonal naive (next Monday = last Monday). MASE measures exactly this.
  • Metrics: MAE and RMSE are scale-dependent. MAPE blows up near zero, so prefer sMAPE, WAPE or MASE for intermittent demand.
  • Tree models can't extrapolate trends. Detrend first or predict differences.
  • Horizon matters: recursive multi-step forecasting compounds errors, while direct or multi-output strategies avoid that.

Each model family in plain English

1 · Exponential smoothing (ETS / Holt-Winters)

🧒 Idea

A weighted average of the past where recent days count more, with the weights fading exponentially. Holt adds a trend, and Holt-Winters adds seasonality on top.

🏆 Wins when…

You have one or a few series with clear trend and seasonality and limited history, and need fast, robust, explainable forecasts, e.g. monthly sales of a product line or weekly call-centre volume. It's a superb baseline that's hard to beat on simple series.

2 · ARIMA / SARIMA / SARIMAX

🧒 Idea

Predict the next value from its own recent values (AR) and recent forecast errors (MA), after differencing (I) to remove the trend. SARIMA adds the same idea at the seasonal lag (e.g. the same month last year), and SARIMAX adds external drivers like price or holidays.

  • Needs a stationary series after differencing, with orders (p, d, q) chosen via ACF/PACF plots and AIC, or automatically (auto_arima).
  • Gives proper prediction intervals and interpretable dynamics.
  • One model per series. Slow for thousands of series, and struggles with multiple seasonalities (daily and weekly and yearly).
🏆 Wins when…

There are few series with strong autocorrelation and a medium history length, and you need statistically sound intervals: economics, finance indicators, energy load for one region. Use SARIMAX when known drivers (price, promotions) exist.

3 · Prophet

🧒 Idea

A “curve-fitting” model: trend (which can bend at changepoints) + weekly and yearly patterns + holiday effects, added together. It was built for analysts who aren't time-series experts.

🏆 Wins when…

Daily business data with several seasonalities, holidays, missing days and outliers, like website traffic or orders, and you need a quick, tunable, explainable forecast. It's often beaten by tuned ETS or GBMs on accuracy, so treat it as a strong, convenient baseline.

4 · ML / gradient boosting with lag features (LightGBM, XGBoost)

🧒 Idea

Turn forecasting into an ordinary table: for each date and series, create features like “sales 7 days ago”, “28-day average”, “day of week”, “price”, “is holiday”, then train one regression model across all series at once (a global model).

  • Learns shared patterns across thousands of series (SKUs × stores) and uses rich external drivers.
  • Needs careful leakage-free features (shift before rolling) and detrending, because trees can't extrapolate.
  • Won the M5 forecasting competition, and is the industry standard for retail demand.
🏆 Wins when…

There are many related series (hundreds to millions), lots of external drivers (price, promotions, weather, events) and enough history. Typical cases: retail and e-commerce demand, delivery volumes, ride demand per zone.

5 · Deep learning (N-BEATS, TFT, DeepAR)

🏆 Wins when…

The data is very large (many long series), patterns are complex, you need probabilistic multi-horizon forecasts, and the team can afford the complexity. Otherwise GBMs usually match it with far less effort.

⚠️ Traps that sink time-series answers
  • Random K-fold or shuffling, which lets the model see the future.
  • Rolling features that include the current value, or features you won't know at forecast time.
  • Reporting MAPE when actual values hit zero.
  • Skipping the naive baseline. Fancy models often lose to it.
💼 Practical example to quote

Grocery demand for 20k SKUs × 300 stores, 14 days ahead: one global LightGBM on lags (7, 14, 28 days), rolling means shifted by the horizon, price, promotion flag, holidays and day-of-week, validated by walk-forward backtests on the last 8 weeks. It beats per-series ARIMA on WAPE, and slow-moving SKUs use a simple ETS fallback.

Components

  • Trend: long-run increase/decrease.
  • Seasonality: fixed-period patterns (weekly, yearly).
  • Cycles: irregular-period fluctuations (business cycles).
  • Noise/residual.

Additive \(y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\) when seasonal amplitude is constant; multiplicative \(y_t=T_t\times S_t\times R_t\) when it grows with the level (log turns it additive). Tools: classical decomposition, STL (robust, flexible).

Stationarity

A (weakly) stationary series has constant mean, constant variance, and autocovariance that depends only on the lag. Most classical models assume it.

TestH₀Reading
ADF (Augmented Dickey–Fuller)Unit root (non-stationary)p < 0.05 → reject → stationary
KPSSStationaryp < 0.05 → reject → non-stationary

Use both (they have opposite nulls). Fixes: differencing \(y_t-y_{t-1}\) (trend), seasonal differencing \(y_t-y_{t-s}\), log / Box-Cox (variance), detrending.

ACF and PACF

  • ACF: correlation of the series with its lag k (includes indirect effects through intermediate lags).
  • PACF: correlation with lag k after removing the effect of lags 1…k−1.
ProcessACFPACF

Exponential smoothing, Prophet & others

  • Simple exponential smoothing: \(\hat y_{t+1}=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\hat y_t\) — level only.
  • Holt adds trend (optionally damped); Holt–Winters adds seasonality (additive/multiplicative). ETS = state-space formulation with error, trend, seasonal components.
  • Prophet: additive model y = trend (piecewise linear/logistic with changepoints) + Fourier seasonalities + holidays; robust to missing data, easy for business series.
  • Croston / TSB for intermittent demand (many zeros).
  • Global ML / DL models: LightGBM on lags across thousands of SKUs; N-BEATS, TFT, DeepAR; foundation models (e.g. TimesFM, Chronos) for zero-shot baselines.

Validation

Cross-validation schemes

  • Match the validation horizon to the business horizon (next 7 days, next quarter).

The ARIMA family

∑ ARIMA(p, d, q)
$$\text{AR}(p):\ y_t=c+\phi_1y_{t-1}+\dots+\phi_py_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t\qquad \text{MA}(q):\ y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\dots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}$$

ARIMA applies ARMA(p, q) to the series differenced d times. SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)ₛ adds seasonal AR/MA/differencing at period s. SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors (price, promotions, holidays).

  • Box–Jenkins: identify (stationarity, ACF/PACF) → estimate (MLE) → diagnose (residuals ≈ white noise: Ljung–Box test) → forecast.
  • Order selection by AIC/BIC (auto_arima).
  • Limits: one series at a time, linear, struggles with multiple seasonalities and many covariates.

Interview questions

What is stationarity and why does it matter?

A weakly stationary series has constant mean and variance over time and autocovariance that depends only on lag. Models like ARMA assume it so that patterns learned from the past apply to the future; non-stationary series can produce spurious correlations and unreliable forecasts. Check with plots, ADF and KPSS; fix with differencing or transforms.

How do you choose p, d, q for ARIMA?

d: number of differences needed for stationarity (ADF/KPSS). p: lag where the PACF cuts off (AR order). q: lag where the ACF cuts off (MA order). Refine by AIC/BIC grid search (auto_arima) and verify residuals are white noise (Ljung–Box).

Why can't you use regular K-fold cross-validation for time series?

Shuffling puts future observations in training and past ones in validation, leaking future information and producing over-optimistic scores; autocorrelated neighbours also leak. Use time-ordered splits (expanding/sliding window, TimeSeriesSplit), with a gap for look-back features.

ACF vs PACF?

ACF at lag k is the correlation between y_t and y_{t−k}, including indirect effects through intermediate lags. PACF at lag k is the correlation after controlling for lags 1…k−1 — the direct effect. AR(p) shows PACF cut-off at p; MA(q) shows ACF cut-off at q.

How would you forecast sales for 10,000 products?

A global model (LightGBM) trained across all series with lag/rolling/calendar/price/promo features and product/store categoricals, predicting scaled or differenced targets; hierarchical reconciliation if forecasts must sum across levels; special handling for intermittent and new (cold-start) products; rolling-origin backtests; WAPE/MASE metrics; baselines per series.

What are the pitfalls of MAPE?

Undefined/infinite when actuals are zero, explodes for small actuals, asymmetric (over-forecasts can be penalised without bound while under-forecasts cap at 100%), and it favours models that under-forecast. Alternatives: WAPE, sMAPE, MASE.

Recursive vs direct multi-step forecasting?

Recursive: one 1-step model, feed predictions back as inputs — simple but errors compound. Direct: separate model per horizon — no compounding but more models and ignores dependencies between horizons. Hybrids and multi-output models sit in between.

How do tree models handle trend in time series?

Poorly — they can't extrapolate beyond the training target range. Remove trend first (differencing, ratio to a rolling mean, detrending with a linear model) and let the trees model the stationary remainder, or combine a linear trend component with tree residual models.

What is a unit root?

In y_t = ρ y_{t−1} + ε_t, ρ = 1 is a unit root: a random walk whose shocks persist forever and variance grows with time — non-stationary. ADF tests H₀: unit root. First differencing turns a random walk into stationary white noise.

How do you handle multiple seasonalities?

Fourier terms for each period as regressors (e.g. daily + weekly + yearly), TBATS, Prophet (multiple seasonal components), MSTL decomposition, or calendar features in ML models.

How do you produce prediction intervals?

From model-based standard errors (ARIMA/ETS), quantile regression (LightGBM quantile objective / pinball loss), bootstrapped residual simulation, or conformal prediction with time-aware calibration. Evaluate with coverage and interval width.
