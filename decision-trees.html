Decision trees

A flowchart of yes/no questions learned from data. On its own it's the most explainable model there is, and it's the building block of Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost. Understand trees well and half of tabular ML follows.

🧒 In plain English

It's the game of “20 questions”. To decide whether a loan applicant is risky, the tree asks “Is income above ₹50k?”. If not, it asks “Missed any payment last year?”, and so on, until it reaches a box that says “risky” or “safe”.

The tree learns which question to ask first by trying every feature and cut-off and picking the one that splits people into the “purest” groups, i.e. groups where almost everyone has the same answer. Then it repeats inside each group. Without limits, it keeps asking until every training person sits in their own box, which is perfect memorisation and poor generalisation.

How it works, step by step (no maths)

  1. Put all training rows in one box (the root).
  2. Try every feature and every possible cut-off, e.g. age < 30, age < 31…
  3. Pick the split that makes the two child boxes most “pure”, measured by Gini or entropy for classes, or by variance for numbers.
  4. Repeat inside each child box. The choice is greedy: the best split right now, without looking ahead.
  5. Stop when a box is pure, too small, or the tree hits max_depth. Optionally prune branches that don't help on validation data.
  6. Predict: drop a new row down the questions. The leaf's majority class (or average value) is the answer.
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“A decision tree recursively splits the data on the feature and threshold that most reduce impurity: Gini or entropy for classification, variance (MSE) for regression. This is CART, a greedy algorithm that builds binary splits. Leaves predict the majority class or the mean. Trees are interpretable, need no feature scaling, handle non-linearity and interactions naturally, and work with mixed feature types. Their weakness is high variance: a fully grown tree memorises noise, and small data changes can produce a completely different tree. So I limit max_depth and min_samples_leaf, or use cost-complexity pruning, and in practice use ensembles: bagging (Random Forest) to cut variance or boosting to cut bias.”

Must-know points for interviews

  • Split quality = impurity reduction. Gini means “how often would I mislabel a random item from this box?”. Entropy means “how surprised am I by the box's contents?”. Both are 0 for a pure box, they agree about 95% of the time, and Gini is slightly faster.
  • Regression trees split to reduce variance and predict the leaf's average, so the prediction is a step function.
  • Greedy and top-down. Finding the perfect tree is computationally infeasible, so each step takes the locally best split.
  • High variance: deep trees overfit, and a small change in the data can change the whole tree. That's the main reason ensembles exist.
  • Control complexity with max_depth, min_samples_leaf, min_samples_split, max_leaf_nodes, or pruning (ccp_alpha).
  • No scaling needed, and outliers in features don't matter, because only the order of values counts.
  • Axis-aligned splits: a diagonal boundary needs many staircase steps.
  • Can't extrapolate. Predictions outside the training range equal the nearest leaf's value.
  • Feature importance (impurity-based) is biased toward high-cardinality features. Use permutation importance or SHAP.

Going deeper: advanced intuition

instability

One different first question changes everything

If two features are nearly tied for the first split, a tiny data change flips the winner and the whole tree below it changes. Averaging many trees (Random Forest) turns this weakness into a strength.

interactions

Interactions for free

A path like “income < 50k AND age < 25” is a learned interaction. Linear models need you to add such terms by hand.

pruning

Grow big, then cut back

Cost-complexity pruning grows a full tree, then removes the branches whose accuracy gain doesn't justify their size, choosing the trade-off by cross-validation. It often beats stopping early, because a weak split can lead to a strong one below it.

high-cardinality bias

Why IDs look “important”

A feature with thousands of unique values (like customer_id) offers thousands of cut-offs, so by chance one looks great. That inflates impurity-based importance and causes overfitting.

probabilities

Leaf proportions are crude

A leaf with 3 rows gives probabilities of 0%, 33%, 67% or 100%, which is poorly calibrated. Larger leaves or ensembles give smoother probabilities.

ancestry

ID3 → C4.5 → CART

ID3 used information gain with multi-way splits. C4.5 added gain ratio and continuous features. CART (used by sklearn) does binary splits with Gini or MSE and cost-complexity pruning.

When a single decision tree wins (and when it doesn't)

🏆 Pick a single tree when…
  • Explainability is the product: business rules you can print on one page, triage flowcharts, policy rules, loan-officer guidelines.
  • You want to discover segments in the data quickly (“who churns?”) or sanity-check features and leakage before modelling seriously.
  • You need extremely fast, dependency-free inference (a few if-statements on an edge device or in SQL).
  • The data mixes types, has non-linear thresholds, and the pattern is genuinely rule-like.

💼 Practical example to quote

Hospital triage guideline: doctors need a rule they can follow at the bedside, e.g. “if SpO₂ < 92% → urgent; else if age > 65 and fever → priority”. A depth-3 tree trained on past cases gives exactly that, readable on one card. A gradient-boosted model might be 3% more accurate but couldn't be used on the ward.

Regression trees

Splits minimise the weighted variance (MSE) of the children: \(\text{MSE}(S)=\frac1{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}(y_i-\bar y_S)^2\). Leaves predict the mean (MAE criterion → median, "poisson" for counts). Predictions are piecewise constant: a tree cannot extrapolate outside the range of training targets.

Overfitting & pruning

  • An unrestricted tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure → memorises noise (training accuracy 100%).
  • Pre-pruning (early stopping): max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes, min_impurity_decrease.
  • Post-pruning: grow fully, then remove branches that don't pay for their complexity. Cost-complexity pruning minimises \(R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha|T|\) (R = training error, |T| = number of leaves). sklearn: cost_complexity_pruning_path gives candidate α's; choose ccp_alpha by CV.
  • Post-pruning is often better because a split that looks useless can enable useful splits below it (e.g., XOR).

Important properties

  • No scaling needed: splits depend only on the order of values (monotonic transforms don't change the tree).
  • Interactions for free: a path of splits is an AND of conditions.
  • Axis-aligned boundaries: a diagonal boundary needs a staircase of many splits.
  • High variance / instability: small data changes can produce a completely different tree.
  • Feature importance (MDI) = total weighted impurity decrease per feature — biased toward high-cardinality/continuous features; prefer permutation importance or SHAP.
  • Missing values: sklearn ≥ 1.3 trees support NaN natively (learns which side missing values go); CART historically used surrogate splits.
  • Categoricals: sklearn needs encoding (one-hot or ordinal); LightGBM/CatBoost handle them natively.

Pros, cons & when to use

✅ Strengths

  • Interpretable rules; easy to visualise & explain to business
  • No scaling, handles mixed types, non-linearity, interactions
  • Fast prediction O(depth)
  • Robust to outliers in features

❌ Weaknesses

  • Overfits; high variance, unstable
  • Greedy, axis-aligned, step-function predictions
  • Can't extrapolate
  • Biased toward dominant classes & many-valued features

Split criteria

∑ Impurity measures (p_k = class share in the node)
$$\text{Gini}=1-\sum_k p_k^2\qquad\text{Entropy}=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\qquad\text{Misclassification}=1-\max_k p_k$$ $$\text{Gain}=I(\text{parent})-\frac{n_L}{n}I(L)-\frac{n_R}{n}I(R)$$
  • Binary node: Gini max 0.5, entropy max 1.0 (both at 50/50); both 0 when pure.
  • Gini is slightly faster (no log) and tends to isolate the most frequent class; entropy gives slightly more balanced trees. In practice they agree on >95% of splits — choose by CV or default to Gini.
  • Misclassification error isn't used for growing because it's not strictly concave — it often shows zero gain for useful splits.
  • Information gain ratio (C4.5) divides by split information to counter ID3's bias toward high-cardinality features.

Interview questions

How does a decision tree decide where to split?

It evaluates every feature and candidate threshold and chooses the split that maximises the impurity decrease (Gini/entropy for classification, variance/MSE reduction for regression), weighted by child sizes. It does this greedily at each node.

Gini impurity vs entropy — which and why?

Both measure node impurity and are maximal at a uniform class mix. Gini = 1 − Σp² (no logarithm, slightly faster); entropy = −Σp log p (information-theoretic, slightly more balanced splits). They produce nearly identical trees; Gini is sklearn's default.

Compute the Gini of a node with 30 positives and 10 negatives.

p = 0.75, 0.25 → 1 − (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. Entropy = −0.75 log₂0.75 − 0.25 log₂0.25 ≈ 0.811.

Why do decision trees overfit and how do you prevent it?

They can keep splitting until every leaf is pure, fitting noise. Prevent with pre-pruning (max_depth, min_samples_leaf, min_samples_split, max_leaf_nodes, min_impurity_decrease), post-pruning (cost-complexity ccp_alpha), or by ensembling (bagging/RF, boosting).

How do regression trees work?

Same recursive partitioning, but splits minimise the weighted MSE (variance) of children, and leaves predict the mean of their targets (median with MAE). The result is a piecewise-constant function that can't extrapolate.

Do decision trees need feature scaling? Why?

No. A split only depends on the ordering of a feature's values, which monotonic transformations (scaling, log) preserve. The same goes for tree ensembles.

What's the difference between pre-pruning and post-pruning?

Pre-pruning stops growth early with constraints (cheap, but may stop before a useful deeper split — horizon effect). Post-pruning grows the full tree and then removes subtrees whose complexity isn't justified by error reduction, e.g. minimal cost-complexity pruning R(T) + α|T| with α chosen by CV.

Why are decision trees unstable / high variance?

Greedy hierarchical splits: a small change in data can change the root split, which changes everything below. This is why averaging many trees (bagging, random forests) dramatically reduces variance.

Why is impurity-based (MDI) feature importance biased?

It's computed on training data and favours features with many possible split points (continuous or high-cardinality), which can reduce impurity by chance — even random IDs get importance. Prefer permutation importance on held-out data or SHAP values.

How do trees handle categorical variables and missing values?

In theory a tree can split a categorical feature into subsets; sklearn needs encoding (ordinal is often fine for trees; one-hot can fragment). LightGBM/CatBoost handle categories natively. Missing values: C4.5 distributes fractionally, CART uses surrogate splits, sklearn ≥ 1.3 / XGBoost / LightGBM learn a default direction for NaNs.

What is the time complexity of training a decision tree?

Roughly O(d · n log n) per level with presorting (sort each feature once, then sweep thresholds), giving O(d · n log n · depth) overall; balanced trees have depth ≈ log n. Prediction is O(depth).

Why can't a tree extrapolate and why does that matter?

Leaves output constants learned from training targets, so beyond the training range the prediction is flat. For trending data (time series with growth, prices rising) trees underpredict — detrend, model differences, or use linear components.

Why isn't misclassification error used to grow trees?

It's piecewise linear (not strictly concave) in class proportions, so many splits that improve purity produce zero change in error and the greedy search gets stuck. Gini and entropy are strictly concave, rewarding any increase in purity.

How would you explain a decision tree to a business stakeholder?

Walk a path as a rule: “If tenure < 6 months AND monthly charges > ₹1,500 AND no contract → 72% churn probability (based on 840 customers).” Show the top few levels, leaf sizes and probabilities.
