Baselines, exponential smoothing & decomposition

Naive, seasonal naive and drift forecasts; simple, Holt and Holt-Winters exponential smoothing; the ETS framework; Theta; and STL, MSTL and TBATS decomposition. These are fast, explainable, and in forecasting competitions they are embarrassingly hard to beat. Interviewers use them to check whether you benchmark before you reach for LSTMs.

🧒 In plain English

To guess tomorrow's café sales, the simplest sensible rules are: “same as today” (naive), “same as last Monday” (seasonal naive), or “today plus the average daily growth” (drift). Exponential smoothing is a smarter version of “recent days matter more”: it keeps a running estimate of the level, nudging it towards each new observation by a fraction α. Add a running estimate of the trend (Holt) and of the weekly pattern (Holt-Winters) and you have a model that big retailers still run on millions of series.

🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“I always start with naive and seasonal-naive baselines and report skill relative to them, for example MASE. Then exponential smoothing: SES for level only, Holt adds a trend (usually damped), Holt-Winters adds seasonality, additive or multiplicative. ETS is the state-space formulation — Error, Trend, Seasonal — which gives likelihoods, AIC model selection and prediction intervals. For multiple or long seasonalities I'd decompose with STL or MSTL, or use TBATS or Fourier regressors. These methods are fast, robust and often win on short or noisy series.”

Simple exponential smoothing (SES): level only

∑ The recursion
$$\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1},\qquad \hat y_{T+h}=\ell_T\ \ (\text{flat for every }h)$$

Unrolling it shows why it's called exponential: \(\ell_T=\alpha y_T+\alpha(1-\alpha)y_{T-1}+\alpha(1-\alpha)^2y_{T-2}+\dots\) — a weighted average whose weights decay geometrically.

Worked example (α = 0.5, start ℓ₀ = 10): y = 12 → ℓ = 0.5·12 + 0.5·10 = 11; y = 9 → ℓ = 10; y = 14 → ℓ = 12. Forecast for every future step: 12.

  • α near 1 → reacts fast, close to naive. α near 0 → very smooth, close to the long-run mean.
  • α is fitted by minimising one-step-ahead squared errors (or by maximum likelihood in ETS).
  • SES is the optimal forecast for an ARIMA(0,1,1) process — a random walk observed with noise.

Holt's linear trend and the damped trend

∑ Level + trend
$$\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\quad b_t=\beta(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta)b_{t-1},\quad \hat y_{T+h}=\ell_T+h\,b_T$$

Damped: \(\hat y_{T+h}=\ell_T+(\phi+\phi^2+\dots+\phi^h)\,b_T\) with 0.8 ≤ φ < 1, so the trend flattens out over the horizon.

A straight trend extended for 24 months is usually too optimistic (or pessimistic). The damped trend is one of the most reliable forecasting methods ever tested — a good default when a trend is present but you don't trust it to continue forever.

Holt-Winters: level + trend + season

∑ Additive Holt-Winters (season length m)
$$\begin{aligned}\ell_t&=\alpha(y_t-s_{t-m})+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\ b_t&=\beta(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta)b_{t-1}\\ s_t&=\gamma(y_t-\ell_{t-1}-b_{t-1})+(1-\gamma)s_{t-m}\\ \hat y_{T+h}&=\ell_T+h\,b_T+s_{T+h-m(k+1)}\end{aligned}$$

Multiplicative seasonality replaces “− s” with “÷ s” and the forecast multiplies by the seasonal index: use it when the seasonal swing grows with the level (or log-transform and use additive).

The ETS taxonomy (Error, Trend, Seasonal)

ETS writes every exponential smoothing method as a state-space model with an explicit error term. That gives a likelihood (so parameters are fitted by MLE), information criteria (AICc) to choose between models automatically, and proper prediction intervals.

ComponentOptionsNotation
ErrorAdditive, MultiplicativeA, M
TrendNone, Additive, Additive dampedN, A, Ad
SeasonalNone, Additive, MultiplicativeN, A, M

Examples: ETS(A,N,N) = SES; ETS(A,A,N) = Holt; ETS(A,Ad,N) = damped Holt; ETS(A,A,A) = additive Holt-Winters; ETS(M,Ad,M) = multiplicative errors, damped trend, multiplicative season. The point forecasts of A and M error versions are the same; their intervals differ (multiplicative errors widen with the level). Multiplicative errors need strictly positive data.

The Theta method

Winner of the M3 competition. Decompose the (deseasonalised) series into two “theta lines”: θ = 0 (the straight regression trend) and θ = 2 (the curvature doubled). Forecast the first by extrapolating the line and the second with SES, then average. Hyndman & Billah showed it is equivalent to SES with drift equal to half the regression slope — a damped-ish trend in disguise. Great to name in an interview as “a simple benchmark that beat sophisticated models”.

Decomposition: classical, STL, MSTL

\(y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t\) (additive) or \(T_t\times S_t\times R_t\) (multiplicative, or additive on logs).

  • Classical: centred moving average for trend; average detrended values by season. Simple, but loses the ends and assumes a fixed seasonal pattern.
  • STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition with Loess): seasonality can change slowly over time, robust to outliers (robust weights), works for any period. Key settings: period, seasonal (smoothness of the seasonal component), robust=True.
  • MSTL: STL applied iteratively for multiple periods (e.g. hourly electricity: daily 24 and weekly 168).
  • X-13ARIMA-SEATS: the official-statistics standard (trading-day and holiday effects).

Strength of trend/seasonality (a great EDA number): \(F_S=\max\!\big(0,\,1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(R)}{\operatorname{Var}(S+R)}\big)\), similarly \(F_T\). Values near 1 mean strong seasonality/trend.

Forecast with decomposition (“STL + ETS” or “STL + ARIMA”): forecast the seasonally adjusted series with any model, then add back the last seasonal cycle (seasonal naive on S).

Must-know points for interviews

  • Always beat the baseline: naive / seasonal naive / drift; report MASE or skill vs baseline.
  • SES = exponentially weighted average; flat forecasts; α controls reactivity.
  • Holt adds trend; damp it (φ ≈ 0.9) for long horizons.
  • Holt-Winters adds seasonality: additive when swings are constant, multiplicative when they scale with level.
  • ETS = state-space version → likelihood, AICc selection, intervals.
  • STL/MSTL for flexible and multiple seasonalities; robust option for outliers.
  • Theta ≈ SES with drift; M3 winner; simple methods are hard to beat.

Interview questions

What baselines do you use for forecasting and why?

Naive (last value), seasonal naive (same period last cycle), drift, and mean. They're free, hard to beat on many real series, and define whether a sophisticated model adds value. Report MASE or relative error vs seasonal naive, per horizon.

Explain simple exponential smoothing.

The forecast is a weighted average of past values with weights decaying geometrically by (1 − α) per step: \(\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}\). Forecasts are flat at the last level. α near 1 reacts quickly, near 0 smooths heavily. It suits series with no trend or seasonality.

Additive or multiplicative seasonality — how do you decide?

Plot it: if the seasonal amplitude is roughly constant as the level changes, additive; if it grows proportionally with the level (e.g. December sales spike is 30% above average whatever the level), multiplicative. Alternatively log-transform, which turns multiplicative into additive, then use additive models and back-transform (with bias adjustment for the mean).

Why use a damped trend?

Linear trend extrapolation over long horizons tends to overshoot because real trends slow down or reverse. Damping (φ < 1) makes the trend contribution converge to a constant. Empirically, damped-trend exponential smoothing is among the most accurate methods in large forecasting competitions.

What is ETS and how does it differ from Holt-Winters?

ETS is the innovations state-space formulation of exponential smoothing (Error, Trend, Seasonal, each N/A/Ad/M). Holt-Winters is the forecasting recursion; ETS adds an explicit error process, so you get a likelihood (MLE fitting), AICc-based automatic model selection across the 30 combinations, and statistically grounded prediction intervals.

How does exponential smoothing relate to ARIMA?

Linear ETS models are special cases of ARIMA: SES ≡ ARIMA(0,1,1), Holt's linear ≡ ARIMA(0,2,2), additive Holt-Winters ≡ a seasonal ARIMA. But there are ARIMA models with no ETS counterpart and multiplicative ETS models with no ARIMA counterpart. ETS models trend and season explicitly; ARIMA models autocorrelation after differencing.

What is STL and when would you use it?

Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess: iteratively smooths to separate trend, seasonal and remainder. Seasonality may evolve over time, any period works, and a robust option down-weights outliers. Use for EDA (strength of trend/season), for anomaly detection on the remainder, and for forecasting by modelling the seasonally adjusted series and adding back the season. MSTL extends it to multiple periods.

How would you handle hourly data with daily and weekly seasonality?

Options: MSTL with periods 24 and 168 plus ETS/ARIMA on the remainder; regression or ARIMA with Fourier terms for each period (few sin/cos pairs instead of hundreds of dummies); TBATS; Prophet; or gradient boosting with hour-of-day, day-of-week and holiday features plus lags. Classical Holt-Winters/SARIMA handle only one seasonal period.

What is the Theta method?

The M3-competition winner: decompose the deseasonalised series into a linear trend line (θ = 0) and a line with doubled local curvature (θ = 2), forecast them by extrapolation and SES respectively, and average. It is equivalent to SES with drift equal to half the trend slope — a strong, simple benchmark.

Why are simple methods so hard to beat in forecasting competitions?

Real series are short, noisy and non-stationary; complex models overfit the past and pay a variance cost, and regime shifts break learned relationships. Simple methods have low variance and make robust assumptions (recent level matters most). Combinations of simple methods reduce variance further. Complex/global ML models win when there are many related series, rich covariates and enough data (as in M5).

What is MASE and why prefer it to MAPE?

Mean Absolute Scaled Error: test MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of the (seasonal) naive forecast. It's scale-free (comparable across series), defined when values are zero (unlike MAPE), symmetric, and interpretable: < 1 beats the naive benchmark. MAPE explodes near zero and penalises over-forecasts more heavily.

How do you produce prediction intervals for exponential smoothing?

Use the ETS state-space form: analytic variance formulas exist for linear (additive-error) models; for multiplicative models, simulate many future sample paths from the fitted model with bootstrapped or Gaussian errors and take quantiles. Validate coverage on backtests — intervals from fitted models are often too narrow because they ignore parameter and model uncertainty.
