Bagging & Random Forest

Many deep decision trees, each trained on a slightly different random slice of the data, voting together. Random Forest is the most reliable “works out of the box” model for tabular data. Interviews probe bagging, why randomness helps, out-of-bag error and feature-importance traps.

🧒 In plain English

Ask one expert and you get one opinion, which might be wildly off. Ask 500 people, each of whom has seen a different random sample of the evidence and was only allowed to look at a few clues at a time, and then average their answers. Individually they're erratic, but their mistakes point in different directions and cancel out. That's the wisdom of crowds.

One decision tree is that erratic expert: very sensitive to the exact data it saw. Random Forest builds hundreds of deliberately different trees and lets them vote. The more different the trees, the better the crowd.

How it works, step by step (no maths)

  1. Make a bootstrap sample: draw n rows with replacement from the training data. About 63% of rows appear (some twice), and the other ~37% are “out-of-bag”.
  2. Grow a deep tree on that sample, but at each split only consider a random subset of features (e.g. √(number of features)). This is the “random” in Random Forest.
  3. Repeat hundreds of times, independently. This can run fully in parallel.
  4. Predict by majority vote (classification) or averaging (regression).
  5. Free validation: score each row using only the trees that never saw it (the out-of-bag estimate).
🎯 Say this in the interview (30 seconds)

“Random Forest is bagging of decision trees plus feature randomness. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample, and at every split it considers only a random subset of features. That decorrelates the trees, so averaging them cuts variance a lot while keeping the low bias of deep trees. It needs little tuning, no scaling, handles non-linearity and interactions, gives a free out-of-bag error estimate, and is hard to overfit by adding trees. Its weaknesses: impurity-based feature importance is biased toward high-cardinality features, so I use permutation importance or SHAP; it's slower and larger at inference than a single tree; and it can't extrapolate. Tuned boosting usually beats it on accuracy.”

Must-know points for interviews

  • Bagging reduces variance, not bias. So use deep, low-bias trees as the members.
  • Feature subsampling (max_features) is the key addition over plain bagging. It stops one strong feature from making every tree look the same.
  • More trees never overfit. The error flattens out, and extra trees only cost time. Overfitting comes from tree depth and leaf size, not tree count.
  • Out-of-bag (OOB) score: a built-in validation estimate with no separate hold-out set needed.
  • Feature importance trap: impurity (MDI) importance favours high-cardinality and continuous features, and splits credit between correlated ones. Prefer permutation importance or SHAP.
  • No scaling, robust to outliers and noise, handles mixed types. It's a great first model.
  • Parallel training (n_jobs=-1). Boosting is inherently sequential.
  • Can't extrapolate, and predictions are averages, so extremes get pulled toward the middle.
  • Imbalance: class_weight="balanced_subsample" or balanced random forest.

Going deeper: advanced intuition

the core law

Correlation limits the crowd

Averaging infinitely many trees removes the “independent” part of their errors, but the part they share remains. So the real job is to make the trees disagree, which is why bootstrapping plus random feature subsets work.

max_features

The diversity dial

Fewer features per split means more diverse but individually weaker trees. All features means plain bagging, with strong but similar trees. √p for classification and p/3 to all features for regression are good starting points.

vs boosting

Parallel crowd vs sequential coaching

RF builds independent deep trees and averages them, which cuts variance and is robust to noise. Boosting builds dependent shallow trees, each fixing the last, which cuts bias, scores higher accuracy and is more sensitive to noise and tuning.

Extra Trees

Even more random

Extremely Randomised Trees pick split thresholds at random instead of searching for the best one. Training is faster and variance lower, at the cost of a little bias. It's often just as accurate.

proximity

Bonus uses

How often two rows land in the same leaf gives a similarity measure, useful for clustering, outlier detection and imputation. Isolation Forest is a random-tree cousin built for anomaly detection.

noisy labels

Why RF tolerates noise

Each mislabelled row affects only the trees whose bootstrap includes it, and gets out-voted. Boosting keeps trying to fix that row, round after round.

When each one wins

Random Forest

🏆 Pick Random Forest when…
  • You want a strong baseline with almost no tuning on tabular data.
  • Labels are noisy or the dataset is small to medium. It's more forgiving than boosting.
  • You want built-in validation (OOB) and easy parallel training.
  • You need a robust model quickly for feature screening, or as a benchmark that boosting must beat.

Plain bagging

🏆 Pick bagging when…
  • You want to stabilise any high-variance model, not only trees (e.g. kNN or a neural net).
  • There are only a few features, so feature subsampling adds little.

Extra Trees

🏆 Pick Extra Trees when…
  • Training speed matters on large data, or the data is very noisy and you want even lower variance.

💼 Practical example to quote

Predictive maintenance on factory sensors: 30k machine-days, 60 noisy sensor features and labels with some mistakes (failures logged late). Random Forest with default settings and OOB evaluation delivers a solid model on day one, and permutation importance shows which sensors matter. Later, a tuned LightGBM gains about 2% recall, but RF stays as the robust fallback.

Out-of-bag (OOB) evaluation

For each row, average the predictions of only the trees that didn't see it in training. The resulting OOB score is close to a cross-validation estimate at no extra training cost (oob_score=True). It's slightly pessimistic for small B since each row is predicted by ~37% of the trees. Not valid for time-series or grouped data (rows aren't independent).

ExtraTrees go further: thresholds are drawn at random (best of random candidates) and by default no bootstrap. Even lower variance and faster training, slightly higher bias. Worth a try alongside RF.

Pros, cons & when to use

✅ Strengths

  • Strong accuracy with little tuning; hard to badly overfit
  • No scaling; handles non-linearity, interactions, mixed features
  • OOB estimate, feature importance, parallel training
  • Robust to outliers and noisy features

❌ Weaknesses

  • Large models, slower inference than a single tree / linear model
  • Less interpretable than one tree
  • Can't extrapolate; struggles with very sparse high-dimensional data
  • Usually a bit below tuned boosting on tabular benchmarks

Why it works: variance of an average

∑ Variance of the ensemble
$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B T_b\Big)=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$$

As B → ∞ the second term vanishes, but the first stays: correlation ρ between trees limits the benefit. Bootstrapping and feature subsampling reduce ρ (at a small cost in individual-tree bias/variance). Bias of the average ≈ bias of one tree, so use low-bias (deep) trees.

Interview questions

How does a Random Forest work?

It trains many decision trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the rows, and at every split considers only a random subset of features. Predictions are averaged (regression) or majority-voted / probability-averaged (classification). Bootstrapping + feature subsampling decorrelate trees so averaging reduces variance.

Why does bagging reduce variance but not bias?

The average of B identically distributed estimators has the same expectation (so bias is unchanged) but variance ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B, which is smaller than σ². Hence bagging helps high-variance, low-bias learners like deep trees, and does little for stable, high-bias models like linear regression.

Why does RF use a random subset of features at each split?

To decorrelate the trees. Without it, strong predictors dominate the top splits of every tree, making them similar (high ρ), and averaging correlated trees removes little variance. Random subsets force diversity, lowering ρ and the ensemble variance.

Can a Random Forest overfit by adding more trees?

No — adding trees reduces the variance term and the generalisation error converges to a limit. Individual deep trees overfit, and the forest can still overfit via tree depth/leaf size on noisy data, but n_estimators itself isn't an overfitting knob (it's a compute knob).

What is the out-of-bag error?

Each tree leaves out ~36.8% of rows. Predicting each row with only the trees that didn't train on it and scoring those predictions gives an internal, nearly unbiased estimate of test error — like CV for free. Invalid with time ordering or grouped data.

What are the most important RF hyperparameters?

max_features (controls decorrelation vs tree strength), n_estimators (enough to stabilise), and tree size controls (min_samples_leaf, max_depth). Also max_samples, class_weight for imbalance. RF is fairly insensitive compared with boosting.

Random Forest vs Gradient Boosting — when would you choose each?

RF: quick robust baseline, noisy labels, little tuning time, parallel training, lower risk of overfitting. Boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost): usually best accuracy on tabular data when tuned with early stopping, handles bias better, but sequential and more sensitive to hyperparameters and noise.

Why is impurity-based feature importance unreliable?

It's computed on training data and rewards features offering many split points — continuous or high-cardinality columns (even random IDs) — and it splits credit arbitrarily among correlated features. Use permutation importance on validation data, drop-column importance, or SHAP.

Does Random Forest need feature scaling or handle missing values?

No scaling needed (tree splits are order-based). Missing values: sklearn's RF supports NaN since v1.4; otherwise impute (median + missing-indicator). Categoricals need encoding in sklearn.

How do you handle class imbalance in Random Forest?

class_weight="balanced" or "balanced_subsample" (weights recomputed per bootstrap), balanced random forest (undersample majority in each bootstrap — imblearn), threshold tuning on predict_proba, and evaluation with PR-AUC.

How does a RF compute predicted probabilities?

sklearn averages each tree's leaf class proportions (soft voting). These tend to be under-confident near 0/1 (averaging pulls toward the middle); calibrate with isotonic/sigmoid if probabilities matter.

Why are fully grown trees used in RF?

Averaging fixes variance, not bias, so each base learner should have low bias. Deep trees are low-bias/high-variance — the ideal ingredient. Limiting depth would add bias that averaging can't remove (though min_samples_leaf can help on noisy targets).

What is ExtraTrees and how is it different?

Extremely Randomised Trees pick split thresholds at random (choosing the best among random candidates) and by default train on the full dataset (no bootstrap). More randomness → lower variance, faster, slightly more bias.
