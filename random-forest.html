Bagging & Random Forest

Average many deep, decorrelated trees to cut variance without adding much bias. Random Forest is the most reliable “it just works” model on tabular data and the comparison point for boosting. Expect: how bagging reduces variance, why random feature subsets, OOB error, feature importance pitfalls, RF vs GBM.

🎯 Say this in the interview

“Random Forest is bagging of decision trees plus feature randomness. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample, and at each split only a random subset of features (max_features, e.g. √d) is considered, which decorrelates the trees. Predictions are averaged (regression) or voted/probability-averaged (classification). Averaging B trees with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ gives variance \(\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\), so lowering ρ is the key. Trees are grown deep (low bias), more trees never overfit — they just plateau — and the ~37% out-of-bag rows give a free validation estimate. It's robust, needs little tuning or preprocessing, but is large, slower at inference, and impurity importances are biased.”

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating)

  1. Draw B bootstrap samples (n rows sampled with replacement).
  2. Train a high-variance base learner (deep tree) on each, independently and in parallel.
  3. Aggregate: average for regression, majority vote or averaged probabilities for classification.

Each bootstrap sample contains about \(1-(1-1/n)^n\approx 1-1/e\approx 63.2\%\) of unique rows; the remaining ~36.8% are out-of-bag for that tree.

🧒 In plain English

Ask 500 people who each saw a slightly different sample of the evidence and were each allowed to look at only a few clues at a time. Individually they're erratic; their average is remarkably stable. That's the “wisdom of crowds” — but only if the crowd's mistakes aren't all the same, which is why RF forces diversity.

What Random Forest adds

Plain bagged trees are highly correlated — a few strong features dominate every tree's top splits. RF considers only a random subset of max_features candidates at each split, so other features get a chance and the trees disagree more. Defaults: \(\sqrt d\) for classification, all features (1.0) in sklearn regression (originally d/3).

Why it works: variance of an average

∑ Variance of the ensemble
$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B T_b\Big)=\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$$

As B → ∞ the second term vanishes, but the first stays: correlation ρ between trees limits the benefit. Bootstrapping and feature subsampling reduce ρ (at a small cost in individual-tree bias/variance). Bias of the average ≈ bias of one tree, so use low-bias (deep) trees.

Out-of-bag (OOB) evaluation

For each row, average the predictions of only the trees that didn't see it in training. The resulting OOB score is close to a cross-validation estimate at no extra training cost (oob_score=True). It's slightly pessimistic for small B since each row is predicted by ~37% of the trees. Not valid for time-series or grouped data (rows aren't independent).

Extremely Randomised Trees

ExtraTrees go further: thresholds are drawn at random (best of random candidates) and by default no bootstrap. Even lower variance and faster training, slightly higher bias. Worth a try alongside RF.

Pros, cons & when to use

✅ Strengths

  • Strong accuracy with little tuning; hard to badly overfit
  • No scaling; handles non-linearity, interactions, mixed features
  • OOB estimate, feature importance, parallel training
  • Robust to outliers and noisy features

❌ Weaknesses

  • Large models, slower inference than a single tree / linear model
  • Less interpretable than one tree
  • Can't extrapolate; struggles with very sparse high-dimensional data
  • Usually a bit below tuned boosting on tabular benchmarks

Interview questions

How does a Random Forest work?

It trains many decision trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the rows, and at every split considers only a random subset of features. Predictions are averaged (regression) or majority-voted / probability-averaged (classification). Bootstrapping + feature subsampling decorrelate trees so averaging reduces variance.

Why does bagging reduce variance but not bias?

The average of B identically distributed estimators has the same expectation (so bias is unchanged) but variance ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B, which is smaller than σ². Hence bagging helps high-variance, low-bias learners like deep trees, and does little for stable, high-bias models like linear regression.

Why does RF use a random subset of features at each split?

To decorrelate the trees. Without it, strong predictors dominate the top splits of every tree, making them similar (high ρ), and averaging correlated trees removes little variance. Random subsets force diversity, lowering ρ and the ensemble variance.

Can a Random Forest overfit by adding more trees?

No — adding trees reduces the variance term and the generalisation error converges to a limit. Individual deep trees overfit, and the forest can still overfit via tree depth/leaf size on noisy data, but n_estimators itself isn't an overfitting knob (it's a compute knob).

What is the out-of-bag error?

Each tree leaves out ~36.8% of rows. Predicting each row with only the trees that didn't train on it and scoring those predictions gives an internal, nearly unbiased estimate of test error — like CV for free. Invalid with time ordering or grouped data.

What are the most important RF hyperparameters?

max_features (controls decorrelation vs tree strength), n_estimators (enough to stabilise), and tree size controls (min_samples_leaf, max_depth). Also max_samples, class_weight for imbalance. RF is fairly insensitive compared with boosting.

Random Forest vs Gradient Boosting — when would you choose each?

RF: quick robust baseline, noisy labels, little tuning time, parallel training, lower risk of overfitting. Boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost): usually best accuracy on tabular data when tuned with early stopping, handles bias better, but sequential and more sensitive to hyperparameters and noise.

Why is impurity-based feature importance unreliable?

It's computed on training data and rewards features offering many split points — continuous or high-cardinality columns (even random IDs) — and it splits credit arbitrarily among correlated features. Use permutation importance on validation data, drop-column importance, or SHAP.

Does Random Forest need feature scaling or handle missing values?

No scaling needed (tree splits are order-based). Missing values: sklearn's RF supports NaN since v1.4; otherwise impute (median + missing-indicator). Categoricals need encoding in sklearn.

How do you handle class imbalance in Random Forest?

class_weight="balanced" or "balanced_subsample" (weights recomputed per bootstrap), balanced random forest (undersample majority in each bootstrap — imblearn), threshold tuning on predict_proba, and evaluation with PR-AUC.

How does a RF compute predicted probabilities?

sklearn averages each tree's leaf class proportions (soft voting). These tend to be under-confident near 0/1 (averaging pulls toward the middle); calibrate with isotonic/sigmoid if probabilities matter.

Why are fully grown trees used in RF?

Averaging fixes variance, not bias, so each base learner should have low bias. Deep trees are low-bias/high-variance — the ideal ingredient. Limiting depth would add bias that averaging can't remove (though min_samples_leaf can help on noisy targets).

What is ExtraTrees and how is it different?

Extremely Randomised Trees pick split thresholds at random (choosing the best among random candidates) and by default train on the full dataset (no bootstrap). More randomness → lower variance, faster, slightly more bias.
